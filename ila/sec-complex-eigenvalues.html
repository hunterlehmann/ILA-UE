    Section 5.5 Complex Eigenvalues

    Objectives

    1. Learn to find complex eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a matrix.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Learn to recognize a rotation-scaling matrix, and compute by how much the matrix rotates and scales.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Understand the geometry of \(2\times 2\) and \(3\times 3\) matrices with a complex eigenvalue.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Recipes: a \(2\times 2\) matrix with a complex eigenvalue is similar to a rotation-scaling matrix, the eigenvector trick for \(2\times 2\) matrices.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Pictures: the geometry of matrices with a complex eigenvalue.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Theorems: the rotation-scaling theorem, the block diagonalization theorem.
      🔗
      🔗
    7. Vocabulary word: rotation-scaling matrix.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    In Section 5.4, we saw that an \(n \times n\) matrix whose characteristic polynomial has \(n\) distinct real roots is diagonalizable: it is similar to a diagonal matrix, which is much simpler to analyze. The other possibility is that a matrix has complex roots, and that is the focus of this section. It turns out that such a matrix is similar (in the \(2\times 2\) case) to a rotation-scaling matrix, which is also relatively easy to understand.
    🔗
    In a certain sense, this entire section is analogous to Section 5.4, with rotation-scaling matrices playing the role of diagonal matrices.
    🔗
    See Appendix A for a review of the complex numbers.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.5.1 Matrices with Complex Eigenvalues

    As a consequence of the Theorem 3 as applied to the characteristic polynomial, we see that:
    🔗

    Insight 5.5.1.

    Every \(n \times n\) matrix has exactly \(n\) complex eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity.
    🔗
    🔗
    We can compute a corresponding (complex) eigenvector in exactly the same way as before: by row reducing the matrix \(A - \lambda I_n\text{.}\) Now, however, we have to do arithmetic with complex numbers.
    🔗

    Example 5.5.2 A \(2\times 2\) matrix.

    Find the complex eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    The characteristic polynomial of \(A\) is
    \begin{gather*} f(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - \Tr(A)\lambda + \det(A) = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda + 2\text{.} \end{gather*}
    The roots of this polynomial are
    \begin{gather*} \lambda = \frac{2\pm\sqrt{4-8}}2 = 1\pm i\text{.} \end{gather*}
    First we compute an eigenvector for \(\lambda = 1+i\text{.}\) We have
    \begin{align*} A-(1+i) I_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1-(1+i) \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1-(1+i) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -i \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp -i \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Now we row reduce, noting that the second row is \(i\) times the first:
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} -i \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp -i \end{pmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{R_2=R_2-iR_1}\; \begin{pmatrix} -i \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{R_1=R_1\div -i}\; \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -i \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The parametric form is \(x = iy\text{,}\) so that an eigenvector is \(v_1={i\choose 1}\text{.}\) Next we compute an eigenvector for \(\lambda=1-i\text{.}\) We have
    \begin{align*} A-(1-i) I_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1-(1-i) \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1-(1-i) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} i \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp i \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Now we row reduce, noting that the second row is \(-i\) times the first:
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} i \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp i \end{pmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{R_2=R_2+iR_1}\; \begin{pmatrix} i \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{R_1=R_1\div i}\; \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp i \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The parametric form is \(x = -iy\text{,}\) so that an eigenvector is \(v_2 = {-i\choose 1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We can verify our answers:
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} i \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \amp= \begin{pmatrix} i-1 \\ i+1 \end{pmatrix} = (1+i)\begin{pmatrix} i \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\\ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} -i \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \amp= \begin{pmatrix} -i-1 \\ -i+1 \end{pmatrix} = (1-i)\begin{pmatrix} -i \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.5.3 A \(3\times 3\) matrix.

    Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, real and complex, of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 4/5 \amp -3/5 \amp 0 \\ 3/5 \amp 4/5 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    We compute the characteristic polynomial by expanding cofactors along the third row:
    \begin{align*} f(\lambda) = \det\begin{pmatrix} 4/5-\lambda \amp -3/5 \amp 0 \\ 3/5 \amp 4-5-\lambda \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 2-\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (2-\lambda)\left(\lambda^2-\frac 85\lambda+1\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
    This polynomial has one real root at \(2\text{,}\) and two complex roots at
    \begin{gather*} \lambda = \frac{8/5\pm\sqrt{64/25-4}}2 = \frac{4\pm 3i}5\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Therefore, the eigenvalues are
    \begin{gather*} \lambda = 2,\quad \frac{4+3i}5,\quad \frac{4-3i}5\text{.} \end{gather*}
    We eyeball that \(v_1 = e_3\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(2\text{,}\) since the third column is \(2e_3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Next we find an eigenvector with eigenvaluue \((4+3i)/5\text{.}\) We have
    \begin{align*} A-\frac{4+3i}5I_3 = \begin{pmatrix} -3i/5 \amp -3/5 \amp 0 \\ 3/5 \amp -3i/5 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 2-(4+3i)/5 \end{pmatrix} \;\xrightarrow[R_2=R_2\times5/3]{R_1=R_1\times -5/3}\; \begin{pmatrix} i \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp -i \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp \frac{6-3i}5 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    We row reduce, noting that the second row is \(-i\) times the first:
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} i \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp -i \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp \frac{6-3i}5 \end{pmatrix} \amp\;\xrightarrow[\hbox to 2cm{}]{R_2=R_2+iR_1}\; \begin{pmatrix} i \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp \frac{6-3i}5 \end{pmatrix}\\ \amp\;\xrightarrow[\hbox to 2cm{}]{R_3=R_3+iR_1}\; \begin{pmatrix} i \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2+i \amp \frac{6-3i}5 \end{pmatrix}\\ \amp\;\xrightarrow[\hbox to 2cm{}]{R_2\longleftrightarrow R_3}\; \begin{pmatrix} i \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2+i \amp \frac{6-3i}5 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\\ \amp\;\xrightarrow[\hbox to 2cm{\tiny\hss$R_2=R_2\div (2+i)$\hss}]{R_1=R_1\div i}\; \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -i \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp \frac{9-12i}{25} \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\\ \amp\;\xrightarrow[\hbox to 2cm{}]{R_1=R_1+iR_2}\; \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp \frac{12+9i}{25} \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp \frac{9-12i}{25} \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The free variable is \(z\text{;}\) the parametric form of the solution is
    \begin{align*} \left\{\begin{alignedat}{2} x \amp {}={} \amp -\dfrac{12+9i}{25}z \amp {} \\ y \amp {}={} \amp -\dfrac{9-12i}{25}z. \amp {} \end{alignedat}\right\text{.} \end{align*}
    Taking \(z=25\) gives the eigenvector
    \begin{gather*} v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -12-9i \\ -9+12i \\ 25 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    A similar calculation (replacing all occurences of \(i\) by \(-i\)) shows that an eigenvector with eigenvalue \((4-3i)/5\) is
    \begin{gather*} v_3 = \begin{pmatrix} -12+9i \\ -9-12i \\ 25 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    We can verify our calculations:
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} 4/5 \amp -3/5 \amp 0 \\ 3/5 \amp 4/5 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -12+9i \\ -9-12i \\ 25 \end{pmatrix} \amp= \begin{pmatrix} -21/5+72i/5 \\ -72/5-21i/5 \\ 20-15i \end{pmatrix} = \frac{4+3i}5\begin{pmatrix} -12+9i \\ -9-12i \\ 25 \end{pmatrix}\\ \begin{pmatrix} 4/5 \amp -3/5 \amp 0 \\ 3/5 \amp 4/5 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -12-9i \\ -9+12i \\ 25 \end{pmatrix} \amp= \begin{pmatrix} -21/5-72i/5 \\ -72/5+21i/5 \\ 20+15i \end{pmatrix} = \frac{4-3i}5\begin{pmatrix} -12-9i \\ -9+12i \\ 25 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    If \(A\) is a matrix with real entries, then its characteristic polynomial has real coefficients, so Note 5 implies that its complex eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs. In the first example, we notice that
    \begin{align*} 1+i \text{ has an eigenvector } \amp v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} i \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\\ 1-i \text{ has an eigenvector } \amp v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -i \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    In the second example,
    \begin{align*} \frac{4+3i}5 \text{ has an eigenvector } \amp v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -12-9i \\ -9+12i \\ 25 \end{pmatrix}\\ \frac{4-3i}5 \text{ has an eigenvector } \amp v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -12+9i \\ -9-12i \\ 25 \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
    In these cases, an eigenvector for the conjugate eigenvalue is simply the conjugate eigenvector (the eigenvector obtained by conjugating each entry of the first eigenvector). This is always true. Indeed, if \(Av=\lambda v\) then
    \begin{gather*} A \bar v = \bar{Av} = \bar{\lambda v} = \bar \lambda \bar v\text{,} \end{gather*}
    which exactly says that \(\bar v\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\bar \lambda\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Insight 5.5.4.

    Let \(A\) be a matrix with real entries. If
    \begin{align*} \lambda \text{ is a complex eigenvalue with eigenvector } \amp v,\\ \text{then } \bar\lambda \text{ is a complex eigenvalue with eigenvector }\amp\bar v\text{.} \end{align*}
    In other words, both eigenvalues and eigenvectors come in conjugate pairs.
    🔗
    🔗
    Since it can be tedious to divide by complex numbers while row reducing, it is useful to learn the following trick, which works equally well for matrices with real entries.
    🔗

    Note 5.5.5 Eigenvector Trick for \(2\times 2\) Matrices.

    Let \(A\) be a \(2\times 2\) matrix, and let \(\lambda\) be a (real or complex) eigenvalue. Then
    \begin{align*} A - \lambda I_2 = \begin{pmatrix} z \amp w \\ \star \amp \star \end{pmatrix} \quad\implies\quad \begin{pmatrix} -w \\ z \end{pmatrix} \text{ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue } \lambda\text{,} \end{align*}
    assuming the first row of \(A-\lambda I_2\) is nonzero.
    🔗
    🔗
    Indeed, since \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue, we know that \(A-\lambda I_2\) is not an invertible matrix. It follows that the rows are collinear (otherwise the determinant is nonzero), so that the second row is automatically a (complex) multiple of the first:
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} z \amp w \\ \star \amp \star \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} z \amp w \\ cz \amp cw \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    It is obvious that \({-w\choose z}\) is in the null space of this matrix, as is \({w\choose -z}\text{,}\) for that matter. Note that we never had to compute the second row of \(A-\lambda I_2\text{,}\) let alone row reduce!
    🔗

    Example 5.5.6 A \(2\times 2\) matrix, the easy way.

    Find the complex eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    Since the characteristic polynomial of a \(2\times 2\) matrix \(A\) is \(f(\lambda) = \lambda^2-\Tr(A)\lambda + \det(A)\text{,}\) its roots are
    \begin{gather*} \lambda = \frac{\Tr(A)\pm\sqrt{\Tr(A)^2-4\det(A)}}2 = \frac{2\pm\sqrt{4-8}}2 = 1\pm i\text{.} \end{gather*}
    To find an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(1+i\text{,}\) we compute
    \begin{align*} A - (1+i)I_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -i \amp -1 \\ \star \amp \star \end{pmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{\text{eigenvector}}\; v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The eigenvector for the conjugate eigenvalue is the complex conjugate:
    \begin{gather*} v_2 = \bar v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In Example 2 we found the eigenvectors \({i\choose 1}\) and \({-i\choose 1}\) for the eigenvalues \(1+i\) and \(1-i\text{,}\) respectively, but in Example 6 we found the eigenvectors \({1\choose -i}\) and \({1\choose i}\) for the same eigenvalues of the same matrix. These vectors do not look like multiples of each other at first—but since we now have complex numbers at our disposal, we can see that they actually are multiples:
    \begin{gather*} -i\begin{pmatrix} i \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{pmatrix} \qquad i\begin{pmatrix} -i \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.5.2 Rotation-Scaling Matrices

    The most important examples of matrices with complex eigenvalues are rotation-scaling matrices, i.e., scalar multiples of rotation matrices.
    🔗

    Definition 5.5.7.

    A rotation-scaling matrix is a \(2\times 2\) matrix of the form
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} a \amp -b \\ b \amp a \end{pmatrix}\text{,} \end{align*}
    where \(a\) and \(b\) are real numbers, not both equal to zero.
    🔗
    🔗
    The following proposition justifies the name.
    🔗

    Proposition 5.5.8.

    Let
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} a \amp -b \\ b \amp a \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
    be a rotation-scaling matrix. Then:
    1. \(A\) is a product of a rotation matrix
      \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta \amp -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta \amp \cos\theta \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{with a scaling matrix} \begin{pmatrix} r \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp r \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The scaling factor \(r\) is
      \begin{gather*} r = \sqrt{\det(A)} = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}\text{.} \end{gather*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The rotation angle \(\theta\) is the counterclockwise angle from the positive \(x\)-axis to the vector \({a\choose b}\text{:}\) \usetikzlibrary{angles}
      🔗
      
      🔗
      🔗
    4. The eigenvalues of \(A\) are \(\lambda = a \pm bi.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Set \(r = \sqrt{\det(A)} = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}\text{.}\) The point \((a/r, b/r)\) has the property that
    \begin{gather*} \left(\frac ar\right)^2 + \left(\frac br\right)^2 = \frac{a^2+b^2}{r^2} = 1\text{.} \end{gather*}

    🔗
    
    🔗

    as desired.
    🔗
    For the last statement, we compute the eigenvalues of \(A\) as the roots of the characteristic polynomial:

    Subsection 5.5.3 Geometry of \(2\times 2\) Matrices with a Complex Eigenvalue

    Let \(A\) be a \(2\times 2\) matrix with a complex, non-real eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{.}\) Then \(A\) also has the eigenvalue \(\bar\lambda\neq\lambda\text{.}\) In particular, \(A\) has distinct eigenvalues, so it is diagonalizable using the complex numbers. We often like to think of our matrices as describing transformations of \(\R^n\) (as opposed to \(\C^n\)). Because of this, the following construction is useful. It gives something like a diagonalization, except that all matrices involved have real entries.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.5.14.

    Let \(A\) be a \(2\times 2\) real matrix with a complex (non-real) eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{,}\) and let \(v\) be an eigenvector. Then \(A = CBC\inv\) for
    \begin{align*} C = \begin{pmatrix} | \amp | \\ \Re(v) \amp \Im(v) \\ | \amp | \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{and} B = \begin{pmatrix} \Re(\lambda) \amp \Im(\lambda) \\ -\Im(\lambda) \amp \Re(\lambda) \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    In particular, \(A\) is similar to a rotation-scaling matrix that scales by a factor of \(|\lambda| = \sqrt{\det(B)}.\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    First we need to show that \(\Re(v)\) and \(\Im(v)\) are linearly independent, since otherwise \(C\) is not invertible. If not, then there exist real numbers \(x,y,\) not both equal to zero, such that \(x\Re(v) + y\Im(v) = 0\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} (y+ix)v \amp= (y+ix)\bigl(\Re(v)+i\Im(v)\bigr)\\ \amp= y\Re(v) - x\Im(v) + \left(x\Re(v) + y\Im(v)\right)i\\ \amp= y\Re(v) - x\Im(v)\text{.} \end{align*}
    Now, \((y+ix)v\) is also an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{,}\) as it is a scalar multiple of \(v\text{.}\) But we just showed that \((y+ix)v\) is a vector with real entries, and any real eigenvector of a real matrix has a real eigenvalue. Therefore, \(\Re(v)\) and \(\Im(v)\) must be linearly independent after all.
    🔗
    Let \(\lambda = a+bi\) and \(v = {x+yi\choose z+wi}\text{.}\) We observe that
    \begin{align*} Av = \lambda v \amp= (a+bi)\begin{pmatrix} x+yi \\ z+wi \end{pmatrix}\\ \amp= \begin{pmatrix} (ax-by)+(ay+bx)i \\ (az-bw)+(aw+bz)i \end{pmatrix}\\ \amp= \begin{pmatrix} ax-by \\ az-bw \end{pmatrix} + i\begin{pmatrix} ay+bx \\ aw+bz \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    On the other hand, we have
    \begin{gather*} A\left(\begin{pmatrix} x \\ z \end{pmatrix} + i\begin{pmatrix} y \\ w \end{pmatrix}\right) = A\begin{pmatrix} x \\ z \end{pmatrix} + iA\begin{pmatrix} y \\ w \end{pmatrix} = A\Re(v) + iA\Im(v)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Matching real and imaginary parts gives
    \begin{gather*} A\Re(v) = \begin{pmatrix} ax-by \\ az-bw \end{pmatrix} \qquad A\Im(v) = \begin{pmatrix} ay+bx \\ aw+bz \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Now we compute \(CBC\inv\Re(v)\) and \(CBC\inv\Im(v)\text{.}\) Since \(Ce_1 = \Re(v)\) and \(Ce_2 = \Im(v)\text{,}\) we have \(C\inv\Re(v) = e_1\) and \(C\inv\Im(v)=e_2\text{,}\) so
    \begin{align*} CBC\inv\Re(v) \amp= CBe_1 = C\begin{pmatrix} a \\ -b \end{pmatrix} = a\Re(v)-b\Im(v)\\ \amp= a\begin{pmatrix} x \\ z \end{pmatrix} - b\begin{pmatrix} y \\ w \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ax-by \\ az-bw \end{pmatrix} = A\Re(v)\\ CBC\inv\Im(v) \amp= CBe_2 = C\begin{pmatrix} b \\ a \end{pmatrix} = b\Re(v)+a\Im(v)\\ \amp= b\begin{pmatrix} x \\ z \end{pmatrix} + a\begin{pmatrix} y \\ w \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ay+bx \\ aw+bz \end{pmatrix} = A\Im(v)\text{.} \end{align*}
    Therefore, \(A\Re(v) = CBC\inv\Re(v)\) and \(A\Im(v) = CBC\inv\Im(v)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since \(\Re(v)\) and \(\Im(v)\) are linearly independent, they form a basis for \(\R^2\text{.}\) Let \(w\) be any vector in \(\R^2\text{,}\) and write \(w = c\Re(v) + d\Im(v)\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} Aw \amp= A\bigl(c\Re(v) + d\Im(v)\bigr)\\ \amp= cA\Re(v) + dA\Im(v)\\ \amp= cCBC\inv\Re(v) + dCBC\inv\Im(v)\\ \amp= CBC\inv\bigl(c\Re(v) + d\Im(v)\bigr)\\ \amp= CBC\inv w\text{.} \end{align*}
    This proves that \(A = CBC\inv\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Here \(\Re\) and \(\Im\) denote the real and imaginary parts, respectively:
    \begin{gather*} \Re(a+bi) = a \quad \Im(a+bi) = b \quad \Re\begin{pmatrix} x+yi \\ z+wi \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ z \end{pmatrix} \quad \Im\begin{pmatrix} x+yi \\ z+wi \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} y \\ w \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    The rotation-scaling matrix in question is the matrix
    \begin{align*} B = \begin{pmatrix} a \amp -b \\ b \amp a \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{with} a = \Re(\lambda),\; b = -\Im(\lambda)\text{.} \end{align*}
    Geometrically, the rotation-scaling theorem says that a \(2\times 2\) matrix with a complex eigenvalue behaves similarly to a rotation-scaling matrix. See Note .
    🔗

    \begin{align*} C' \amp= \begin{pmatrix} \Re\begin{pmatrix} \amp 2 \amp \\ \amp 1-i \amp \end{pmatrix} \amp \Im\begin{pmatrix} \amp 2 \amp \\ \amp 1-i \amp \end{pmatrix} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\\ B' \amp= \begin{pmatrix} \Re(\bar\lambda) \amp \Im(\bar\lambda) \\ -\Im(\bar\lambda) \amp \Re(\bar\lambda) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -\sqrt3 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp -\sqrt3 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    So, \(A\) is also similar to a clockwise rotation by \(5\pi/6\text{,}\) followed by a scale by \(2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We saw in the above examples that the Theorem 14 can be applied in two different ways to any given matrix: one has to choose one of the two conjugate eigenvalues to work with. Replacing \(\lambda\) by \(\bar\lambda\) has the effect of replacing \(v\) by \(\bar v\text{,}\) which just negates all imaginary parts, so we also have \(A=C'B'(C')\inv\) for
    \begin{align*} C' = \begin{pmatrix} | \amp | \\ \Re(v) \amp -\Im(v) \\ | \amp | \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{and} B' = \begin{pmatrix} \Re(\lambda) \amp -\Im(\lambda) \\ \Im(\lambda) \amp \Re(\lambda) \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The matrices \(B\) and \(B'\) are similar to each other. The only difference between them is the direction of rotation, since \({\Re(\lambda)\choose -\Im(\lambda)}\) and \({\Re(\lambda)\choose \Im(\lambda)}\) are mirror images of each other over the \(x\)-axis: \usetikzlibrary{angles}

    Remark 5.5.27 Classification of \(2\times 2\) matrices up to similarity.

    At this point, we can write down the “simplest” possible matrix which is similar to any given \(2\times 2\) matrix \(A\text{.}\) There are four cases:
    1. \(A\) has two real eigenvalues \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\text{.}\) In this case, \(A\) is diagonalizable, so \(A\) is similar to the matrix
      \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \lambda_2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
      This representation is unique up to reordering the eigenvalues.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(A\) has one real eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of geometric multiplicity \(2\text{.}\) In this case, we saw in Example 48 of Section 5.4 that \(A\) is equal to the matrix
      \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} \lambda \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \lambda \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(A\) has one real eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of geometric multiplicity \(1\text{.}\) In this case, \(A\) is not diagonalizable, and we saw in Remark 26 of Section 5.4 that \(A\) is similar to the matrix
      \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} \lambda \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp \lambda \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(A\) has no real eigenvalues. In this case, \(A\) has a complex eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{,}\) and \(A\) is similar to the rotation-scaling matrix
      \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} \Re(\lambda) \amp \Im(\lambda) \\ -\Im(\lambda) \amp \Re(\lambda) \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
      by the Theorem 14. By Proposition 8, the eigenvalues of a rotation-scaling matrix \(\smallmat a{-b}ba\) are \(a\pm bi\text{,}\) so that two rotation-scaling matrices \(\smallmat a{-b}ba\) and \(\smallmat c{-d}dc\) are similar if and only if \(a=c\) and \(b=\pm d\text{.}\)
      🔗

    Subsection 5.5.4 Block Diagonalization

    For matrices larger than \(2\times 2\text{,}\) there is a theorem that combines the Theorem 7 of Section 5.4 and the Theorem 14. It says essentially that a matrix is similar to a matrix with parts that look like a diagonal matrix, and parts that look like a rotation-scaling matrix.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.5.28.

    Let \(A\) be a real \(n\times n\) matrix. Suppose that for each (real or complex) eigenvalue, the algebraic multiplicity equals the geometric multiplicity. Then \(A = CBC\inv\text{,}\) where \(B\) and \(C\) are as follows:
    • The matrix \(B\) is block diagonal, where the blocks are \(1\times 1\) blocks containing the real eigenvalues (with their multiplicities), or \(2\times 2\) blocks containing the matrices
      \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} \Re(\lambda) \amp \Im(\lambda) \\ -\Im(\lambda) \amp \Re(\lambda) \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
      for each non-real eigenvalue \(\lambda\) (with multiplicity).
      🔗
      🔗
    • The columns of \(C\) form bases for the eigenspaces for the real eigenvectors, or come in pairs \(\bigl(\,\Re(v)\;\Im(v)\,\bigr)\) for the non-real eigenvectors.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The Theorem 28 is proved in the same way as the Theorem 7 of Section 5.4 and the Theorem 14. It is best understood in the case of \(3\times 3\) matrices.
    🔗

    Example 5.5.29 Block Diagonalization of a \(3\times 3\) Matrix with a Complex Eigenvalue.

    Let \(A\) be a \(3\times 3\) matrix with a complex eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\text{.}\) Then \(\bar\lambda_1\) is another eigenvalue, and there is one real eigenvalue \(\lambda_2\text{.}\) Since there are three distinct eigenvalues, they have algebraic and geometric multiplicity one, so the Theorem 28 applies to \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let \(v_1\) be a (complex) eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\text{,}\) and let \(v_2\) be a (real) eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\lambda_2\text{.}\) Then the Theorem 28 says that \(A = CBC\inv\) for
    \begin{align*} C = \begin{pmatrix} \mhp{|} \amp \mhp{|} \amp \mhg{|} \\ \mhp{\Re(v_1)} \amp \mhp{\Im(v_1)} \amp \amp v_2 \\ \mhp{|} \amp \mhp{|} \amp \mhg{|} \end{pmatrix} \qquad B = \begin{tikzpicture}[baseline=(A-2-1.base)] \matrix[math matrix, column sep=0, row sep=0, nodes={inner ysep=2pt, inner xsep=4pt}] (A) { |[seq-blue]| \phantom{-}\Re(\lambda_1) \& |[seq-blue]| \Im(\lambda_1) \& |[black!40]| 0 \\ |[seq-blue]| -\Im(\lambda_1) \& |[seq-blue]| \Re(\lambda_1) \& |[black!40]| 0 \\ |[black!40]| 0 \& |[black!40]| 0 \& |[seq-green]| \lambda_2 \\ }; \draw[thick, seq-blue] (A-1-1.north west) rectangle (A-2-2.south east); \draw[thick, seq-green] (A-2-2.south east) rectangle (A-3-3.south east); \end{tikzpicture}. \end{align*}

    Example 5.5.30 Geometry of a \(3\times 3\) matrix with a complex eigenvalue.

    What does the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \frac 1{29}\begin{pmatrix} 33 \amp -23 \amp 9 \\ 22 \amp 33 \amp -23 \\ 19 \amp 14 \amp 50 \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
    do geometrically?
    🔗

    Solution.

    First we find the (real and complex) eigenvalues of \(A\text{.}\) We compute the characteristic polynomial using whatever method we like:
    \begin{gather*} f(\lambda) = \det(A-\lambda I_3) = -\lambda^3 + 4\lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 4\text{.} \end{gather*}
    We search for a real root using the rational root theorem. The possible rational roots are \(\pm 1,\pm 2,\pm 4\text{;}\) we find \(f(2) = 0\text{,}\) so that \(\lambda-2\) divides \(f(\lambda)\text{.}\) Performing polynomial long division gives
    \begin{gather*} f(\lambda) = -(\lambda-2)\bigl(\lambda^2-2\lambda+2\bigr)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    The quadratic term has roots
    \begin{gather*} \lambda = \frac{2\pm\sqrt{4-8}}2 = 1\pm i\text{,} \end{gather*}
    so that the complete list of eigenvalues is \(\lambda_1 = 1-i\text{,}\) \(\bar\lambda_1 = 1+i\text{,}\) and \(\lambda_2 = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now we compute some eigenvectors, starting with \(\lambda_1=1-i\text{.}\) We row reduce (probably with the aid of a computer):
    \begin{align*} A-(1-i)I_3 = \frac 1{29}\begin{pmatrix} 4+29i \amp -23 \amp 9 \\ 22 \amp 4+29i \amp -23 \\ 19 \amp 14 \amp 21+29i \end{pmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{\text{RREF}}\; \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 7/5+i/5 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -2/5+9i/5 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The free variable is \(z\text{,}\) and the parametric form is
    \begin{align*} \left\{\begin{alignedat}{2} x \amp {}={} \amp -\left(\dfrac 75+\dfrac 15i\right)z \amp {} \\ y \amp {}={} \amp \left(\dfrac 25-\dfrac 95i\right)z \amp {} \end{alignedat}\right. \quad\xrightarrow[\text{eigenvector}]{z=5}\quad v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -7-i \\ 2-9i \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    For \(\lambda_2=2\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{align*} A - 2I_3 = \frac 1{29}\begin{pmatrix} -25 \amp -23 \amp 9 \\ 22 \amp -25 \amp -23 \\ 19 \amp 14 \amp -8 \end{pmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{\text{RREF}}\; \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp -2/3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1/3 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The free variable is \(z\text{,}\) and the parametric form is
    \begin{align*} \left\{\begin{alignedat}{2} x \amp {}={} \amp \dfrac 23z \amp {} \\ y \amp {}={} \amp -\dfrac 13z \amp {} \end{alignedat}\right. \quad\xrightarrow[\text{eigenvector}]{z=3}\quad v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    According to the Theorem 28, we have \(A=CBC\inv\) for
    \begin{align*} C \amp= \begin{pmatrix} | \amp | \amp | \\ \Re(v_1) \amp \Im(v_1) \amp v_2 \\ | \amp | \amp | \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -7 \amp -1 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp -9 \amp -1 \\ 5 \amp 0 \amp 3 \end{pmatrix}\\ B \amp= \begin{pmatrix} \Re(\lambda_1) \amp \Im(\lambda_1) \amp 0 \\ -\Im(\lambda_1) \amp \Re(\lambda_1) \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The matrix \(B\) is a combination of the rotation-scaling matrix \(\smallmat1{-1}11\) from Example 9, and a diagonal matrix. More specifically, \(B\) acts on the \(xy\)-coordinates by rotating counterclockwise by \(45^\circ\) and scaling by \(\sqrt2\text{,}\) and it scales the \(z\)-coordinate by \(2\text{.}\) This means that points above the \(xy\)-plane spiral out away from the \(z\)-axis and move up, and points below the \(xy\)-plane spiral out away from the \(z\)-axis and move down.
    🔗
    The matrix \(A\) does the same thing as \(B\text{,}\) but with respect to the \(\{\Re(v_1),\Im(v_1),v_2\}\)-coordinate system. That is, \(A\) acts on the \(\Re(v_1),\Im(v_1)\)-plane by spiraling out, and \(A\) acts on the \(v_2\)-coordinate by scaling by a factor of \(2\text{.}\) See the demo below.

    1.

      If \(v\) is an eigenvector for \(A\) and \(v\) has complex entries, then \(i \cdot v\) is also an eigenvector for \(A\text{.}\)

    If \(A\) is a \(3 \times 3\) matrix with real entries, what are the possibilities for the number of different non-real eigenvalues of \(A\text{?}\)

    Consider the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix}3 \amp -7 \\ 1 \amp -1\end{pmatrix} \end{align*}

    6.

    Find the complex eigenvalues and a vector in each eigenspace for the following matrix.
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix}3 \amp -2 \\ 4 \amp -1\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The characteristic equation is
    \begin{gather*} 0 = \det(A-\lambda I) = \lambda^2 - \Tr(A) I + \det(A) = \lambda^2 - 2 \lambda + 5\text{,} \end{gather*}
    so the eigenvalues are
    \begin{gather*} \lambda = \dfrac{2 \pm \sqrt{(-2)^2-5(4)}}{2} = \dfrac{2 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2} = \dfrac{2 \pm 4i}{2} = 1 \pm 2i\text{.} \end{gather*}
    We use the \(2 \times 2\) eigenvector trick: if \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) and \((a \ b)\) is the first row of \(A-\lambda I_2\text{,}\) then \(\begin{pmatrix}-b \\ a\end{pmatrix}\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) unless \(a\) and \(b\) are both \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For \(\lambda_1 = 1 + 2i\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{r|r}A-(1+2i)I \amp 0\end{array}\right) = \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}2-2i \amp -2 \amp 0 \\ (*) \amp (*) \amp 0\end{array}\right) = \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}a \amp b \amp 0 \\ (*) \amp (*) \amp 0\end{array}\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
    Therefore, \(v_1 = \begin{pmatrix}-b \\ a\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 2-2i\end{pmatrix}\) is an eigenvector for \(\lambda_1 = 1+2i\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We do not need to do any work to find an eigenvector for the other eigenvalue: \(v_2 = \overline{v_1} = \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 2+2i\end{pmatrix}\) is an eigenvector for \(\lambda_2 = \overline{\lambda_1} = 1-2i\text{.}\)
    🔗
    There are many possible answers for \(v_1\) and \(v_2\text{,}\) and it is generally difficult to tell whether vectors are complex scalar multiples of each other. If you obtained different answers than this solution did for (say) \(v_1\text{,}\) you can check whether your answer is correct by computing \(Av_1\) and seeing if it is equal to \((1+2i)v_1\text{.}\)
