An equation involving vectors with \(n\) coordinates is the same as \(n\) equations involving only numbers. For example, the equation
\begin{gather*} x\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} + y \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 16 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \end{gather*}
simplifies to
\begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ 2x \\ 6x \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} -y \\ -2y \\ -y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 16 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{or} \left(\begin{alignedat}{2} x \amp {}-{} \amp y \amp {} \\ 2x \amp {}-{} \amp 2y \amp {} \\ 6x \amp {}-{} \amp y \amp {} \end{alignedat}\right) = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 16 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
For two vectors to be equal, all of their coordinates must be equal, so this is just the system of linear equations
\begin{align*} \left\{\begin{alignedat}{3} x \amp {}-{} \amp y \amp {}={} \amp 8 \amp {} \\ 2x \amp {}-{} \amp 2y \amp {}={} \amp 16 \amp {} \\ 6x \amp {}-{} \amp y \amp {}={} \amp 3 \amp . \end{alignedat}\right. \end{align*}
