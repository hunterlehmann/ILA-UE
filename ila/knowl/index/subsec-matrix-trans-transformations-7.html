Example 3.1.19 Functions of several variables.

Here is an example of a function from \(\R^2\) to \(\R^3\text{:}\)
\begin{gather*} f\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x+y \\ \cos(y) \\ y-x^2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
The inputs of \(f\) each have two entries, and the outputs have three entries. In this case, we have defined \(f\) by a formula, so we evaluate \(f\) by substituting values for the variables:
\begin{gather*} f\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2+3 \\ \cos(3) \\ 3-2^2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ \cos(3) \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
🔗
Here is an example of a function from \(\R^3\) to \(\R^3\text{:}\)
\begin{gather*} f(v) = \left(\,\parbox{5.5cm}{the counterclockwise rotation of $v$ by an angle of $42^\circ$ about the $z$-axis}\,\right)\text{.} \end{gather*}
In other words, \(f\) takes a vector with three entries, then rotates it; hence the ouput of \(f\) also has three entries. In this case, we have defined \(f\) by a geometric rule.
