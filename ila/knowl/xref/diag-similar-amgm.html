Theorem 5.4.50.

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be similar \(n\times n\) matrices, and let \(\lambda\) be an eigenvalue of \(A\) and \(B\text{.}\) Then:
  1. The algebraic multiplicity of \(\lambda\) is the same for \(A\) and \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  2. The geometric multiplicity of \(\lambda\) is the same for \(A\) and \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
🔗
🔗

Proof.
Since \(A\) and \(B\) have the same characteristic polynomial, the multiplicity of \(\lambda\) as a root of the characteristic polynomial is the same for both matrices, which proves the first statement. For the second, suppose that \(A = CBC\inv\) for an invertible matrix \(C\text{.}\) By Theorem 20 of Section 5.3, the matrix \(C\) takes eigenvectors of \(B\) to eigenvectors of \(A\text{,}\) both with eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{.}\)
🔗
Let \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k\}\) be a basis of the \(\lambda\)-eigenspace of \(B\text{.}\) We claim that \(\{Cv_1,Cv_2,\ldots,Cv_k\}\) is linearly independent. Suppose that
\begin{gather*} c_1Cv_1 + c_2Cv_2 + \cdots + c_kCv_k = 0\text{.} \end{gather*}
Regrouping, this means
\begin{gather*} C\bigl(c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_kv_k\bigr) = 0\text{.} \end{gather*}
By the Theorem 31 of Section 5.1, the null space of \(C\) is trivial, so this implies
\begin{gather*} c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_kv_k = 0\text{.} \end{gather*}
Since \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k\) are linearly independent, we get \(c_1=c_2=\cdots=c_k=0\text{,}\) as desired.
🔗
By the previous paragraph, the dimension of the \(\lambda\)-eigenspace of \(A\) is greater than or equal to the dimension of the \(\lambda\)-eigenspace of \(B\text{.}\) By symmetry (\(B\) is similar to \(A\) as well), the dimensions are equal, so the geometric multiplicities coincide.
