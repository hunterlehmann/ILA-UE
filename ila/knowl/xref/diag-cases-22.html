Example 5.4.48 Diagonalizability of \(2\times 2\) Matrices.

Let \(A\) be a \(2\times 2\) matrix. There are four cases:
  1. \(A\) has two different eigenvalues. In this case, each eigenvalue has algebraic and geometric multiplicity equal to one. This implies \(A\) is diagonalizable. For example:
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 7 \\ 0 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
  2. \(A\) has one eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of algebraic and geometric multiplicity \(2\text{.}\) To say that the geometric multiplicity is \(2\) means that \(\Nul(A-\lambda I_2) = \R^2\text{,}\) i.e., that every vector in \(\R^2\) is in the null space of \(A-\lambda I_2\text{.}\) This implies that \(A-\lambda I_2\) is the zero matrix, so that \(A\) is the diagonal matrix \(\lambda I_2\text{.}\) In particular, \(A\) is diagonalizable. For example:
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
  3. \(A\) has one eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of algebraic multiplicity \(2\) and geometric multiplicity \(1\text{.}\) In this case, \(A\) is not diagonalizable, by part 3 of the Theorem 46. For example, a shear:
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
  4. \(A\) has no eigenvalues. This happens when the characteristic polynomial has no real roots. In particular, \(A\) is not diagonalizable. For example, a rotation:
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
