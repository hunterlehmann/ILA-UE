Theorem 5.5.14.

Let \(A\) be a \(2\times 2\) real matrix with a complex (non-real) eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{,}\) and let \(v\) be an eigenvector. Then \(A = CBC\inv\) for
\begin{align*} C = \begin{pmatrix} | \amp | \\ \Re(v) \amp \Im(v) \\ | \amp | \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{and} B = \begin{pmatrix} \Re(\lambda) \amp \Im(\lambda) \\ -\Im(\lambda) \amp \Re(\lambda) \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
In particular, \(A\) is similar to a rotation-scaling matrix that scales by a factor of \(|\lambda| = \sqrt{\det(B)}.\)
🔗
🔗

Proof.
First we need to show that \(\Re(v)\) and \(\Im(v)\) are linearly independent, since otherwise \(C\) is not invertible. If not, then there exist real numbers \(x,y,\) not both equal to zero, such that \(x\Re(v) + y\Im(v) = 0\text{.}\) Then
\begin{align*} (y+ix)v \amp= (y+ix)\bigl(\Re(v)+i\Im(v)\bigr)\\ \amp= y\Re(v) - x\Im(v) + \left(x\Re(v) + y\Im(v)\right)i\\ \amp= y\Re(v) - x\Im(v)\text{.} \end{align*}
Now, \((y+ix)v\) is also an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{,}\) as it is a scalar multiple of \(v\text{.}\) But we just showed that \((y+ix)v\) is a vector with real entries, and any real eigenvector of a real matrix has a real eigenvalue. Therefore, \(\Re(v)\) and \(\Im(v)\) must be linearly independent after all.
🔗
Let \(\lambda = a+bi\) and \(v = {x+yi\choose z+wi}\text{.}\) We observe that
\begin{align*} Av = \lambda v \amp= (a+bi)\begin{pmatrix} x+yi \\ z+wi \end{pmatrix}\\ \amp= \begin{pmatrix} (ax-by)+(ay+bx)i \\ (az-bw)+(aw+bz)i \end{pmatrix}\\ \amp= \begin{pmatrix} ax-by \\ az-bw \end{pmatrix} + i\begin{pmatrix} ay+bx \\ aw+bz \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
On the other hand, we have
\begin{gather*} A\left(\begin{pmatrix} x \\ z \end{pmatrix} + i\begin{pmatrix} y \\ w \end{pmatrix}\right) = A\begin{pmatrix} x \\ z \end{pmatrix} + iA\begin{pmatrix} y \\ w \end{pmatrix} = A\Re(v) + iA\Im(v)\text{.} \end{gather*}
Matching real and imaginary parts gives
\begin{gather*} A\Re(v) = \begin{pmatrix} ax-by \\ az-bw \end{pmatrix} \qquad A\Im(v) = \begin{pmatrix} ay+bx \\ aw+bz \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
🔗
Now we compute \(CBC\inv\Re(v)\) and \(CBC\inv\Im(v)\text{.}\) Since \(Ce_1 = \Re(v)\) and \(Ce_2 = \Im(v)\text{,}\) we have \(C\inv\Re(v) = e_1\) and \(C\inv\Im(v)=e_2\text{,}\) so
\begin{align*} CBC\inv\Re(v) \amp= CBe_1 = C\begin{pmatrix} a \\ -b \end{pmatrix} = a\Re(v)-b\Im(v)\\ \amp= a\begin{pmatrix} x \\ z \end{pmatrix} - b\begin{pmatrix} y \\ w \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ax-by \\ az-bw \end{pmatrix} = A\Re(v)\\ CBC\inv\Im(v) \amp= CBe_2 = C\begin{pmatrix} b \\ a \end{pmatrix} = b\Re(v)+a\Im(v)\\ \amp= b\begin{pmatrix} x \\ z \end{pmatrix} + a\begin{pmatrix} y \\ w \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ay+bx \\ aw+bz \end{pmatrix} = A\Im(v)\text{.} \end{align*}
Therefore, \(A\Re(v) = CBC\inv\Re(v)\) and \(A\Im(v) = CBC\inv\Im(v)\text{.}\)
🔗
Since \(\Re(v)\) and \(\Im(v)\) are linearly independent, they form a basis for \(\R^2\text{.}\) Let \(w\) be any vector in \(\R^2\text{,}\) and write \(w = c\Re(v) + d\Im(v)\text{.}\) Then
\begin{align*} Aw \amp= A\bigl(c\Re(v) + d\Im(v)\bigr)\\ \amp= cA\Re(v) + dA\Im(v)\\ \amp= cCBC\inv\Re(v) + dCBC\inv\Im(v)\\ \amp= CBC\inv\bigl(c\Re(v) + d\Im(v)\bigr)\\ \amp= CBC\inv w\text{.} \end{align*}
This proves that \(A = CBC\inv\text{.}\)
