    Section 5.3 Similarity

    Objectives

    1. Learn to interpret similar matrices geoemetrically.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Understand the relationship between the eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and characteristic polynomials of similar matrices.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Recipe: compute \(Ax\) in terms of \(B,C\) for \(A=CBC\inv\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Picture: the geometry of similar matrices.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Vocabulary word: similarity.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Some matrices are easy to understand. For instance, a diagonal matrix
    \begin{align*} D = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1/2 \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
    just scales the coordinates of a vector: \(D{x\choose y} = {2x\choose y/2}\text{.}\) The purpose of most of the rest of this chapter is to understand complicated-looking matrices by analyzing to what extent they “behave like” simple matrices. For instance, the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \frac 1{10}\begin{pmatrix} 11 \amp 6 \\ 9 \amp 14 \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
    has eigenvalues \(2\) and \(1/2\text{,}\) with corresponding eigenvectors \(v_1 = {2/3\choose 1}\) and \(v_2 = {-1\choose 1}\text{.}\) Notice that
    \begin{align*} \left.\begin{alignedat}{3} D(xe_1 \amp {}+{} \amp ye_2) \amp {}={} \amp xDe_1+yDe_2 = 2xe_1-\frac12ye_2 \amp {} \\ A(xv_1 \amp {}+{} \amp yv_2) \amp {}={} \amp xAv_1+yAv_2\hfil, = 2xv_1-\frac12yv_2 \amp . \end{alignedat}\right. \end{align*}
    Using \(v_1,v_2\) instead of the usual coordinates makes \(A\) “behave” like a diagonal matrix.
    🔗
    The other case of particular importance will be matrices that “behave” like a rotation matrix: indeed, this will be crucial for understanding Section 5.5 geometrically. See Note .
    🔗
    In this section, we study in detail the situation when two matrices behave similarly with respect to different coordinate systems. In Section 5.4 and Section 5.5, we will show how to use eigenvalues and eigenvectors to find a simpler matrix that behaves like a given matrix.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.1 Similar Matrices

    We begin with the algebraic definition of similarity.
    🔗

    Definition 5.3.2.

    Two \(n\times n\) matrices \(A\) and \(B\) are similar if there exists an invertible \(n\times n\) matrix \(C\) such that \(A = CBC\inv\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.3.3.

    The matrices
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} -12 \amp 15 \\ -10 \amp 13 \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{and} \begin{pmatrix} 3 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -2 \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
    are similar because
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} -12 \amp 15 \\ -10 \amp 13 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 3 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -2 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\inv\text{,} \end{align*}
    as the reader can verify.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.3.4.

    The matrices
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} 3 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -2 \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{and} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
    are not similar. Indeed, the second matrix is the identity matrix \(I_2\text{,}\) so if \(C\) is any invertible \(2\times 2\) matrix, then
    \begin{align*} CI_2C\inv = CC\inv = I_2 \neq \begin{pmatrix} 3 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    As in the above example, one can show that \(I_n\) is the only matrix that is similar to \(I_n\text{,}\) and likewise for any scalar multiple of \(I_n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Similarity is unrelated to row equivalence. Any invertible matrix is row equivalent to \(I_n\text{,}\) but \(I_n\) is the only matrix similar to \(I_n\text{.}\) For instance,
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{and} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
    are row equivalent but not similar.
    🔗
    🔗
    As suggested by its name, similarity is what is called an equivalence relation. This means that it satisfies the following properties.
    🔗

    Proposition 5.3.5.

    Let \(A,B\text{,}\) and \(C\) be \(n\times n\) matrices.
    1. Reflexivity: \(A\) is similar to itself.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Symmetry: if \(A\) is similar to \(B\text{,}\) then \(B\) is similar to \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Transitivity: if \(A\) is similar to \(B\) and \(B\) is similar to \(C\text{,}\) then \(A\) is similar to \(C\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. Taking \(C = I_n = I_n\inv\text{,}\) we have \(A = I_n A I_n\inv\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose that \(A = CBC\inv.\) Multiplying both sides on the left by \(C\inv\) and on the right by \(C\) gives
      \begin{gather*} C\inv A C = C\inv(CBC\inv)C = B\text{.} \end{gather*}
      Since \((C\inv)\inv = C\text{,}\) we have \(B = C\inv A (C\inv)\inv\text{,}\) so that \(B\) is similar to \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Suppose that \(A = DBD\inv\) and \(B = ECE\inv\text{.}\) Subsituting for \(B\) and remembering that \((DE)\inv = E\inv D\inv\text{,}\) we have
      \begin{gather*} A = D(ECE\inv)D\inv = (DE)C(DE)\inv\text{,} \end{gather*}
      which shows that \(A\) is similar to \(C\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.3.6.

    The matrices
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} -12 \amp 15 \\ -10 \amp 13 \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{and} \begin{pmatrix} 3 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -2 \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
    are similar, as we saw in Example 3. Likewise, the matrices
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} -12 \amp 15 \\ -10 \amp 13 \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{and} \begin{pmatrix} -12 \amp 5 \\ -30 \amp 13 \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
    are similar because
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} -12 \amp 5 \\ -30 \amp 13 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} -12 \amp 15 \\ -10 \amp 13 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\inv\text{.} \end{align*}
    It follows that
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} -12 \amp 5 \\ -30 \amp 13 \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{and} \begin{pmatrix} 3 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -2 \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
    are similar to each other.
    🔗
    🔗
    We conclude with an observation about similarity and powers of matrices.
    🔗

    Fact 5.3.7.

    Let \(A = CBC\inv\text{.}\) Then for any \(n\geq 1\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{gather*} A^n = CB^n C\inv\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    First note that
    \begin{gather*} A^2 = AA = (CBC\inv)(CBC\inv) = CB(C\inv C)BC\inv = CBI_nBC\inv = CB^2C\inv\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Next we have
    \begin{gather*} A^3 = A^2A = (CB^2C\inv)(CBC\inv) = CB^2(C\inv C)BC\inv = CB^3C\inv\text{.} \end{gather*}
    The pattern is clear.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.3.8.

    Compute \(A^{100}\text{,}\) where
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \amp 13 \\ -2 \amp -5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} -2 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\inv\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    By the Fact 7, we have
    \begin{align*} A^{100} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}^{100}\begin{pmatrix} -2 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\inv\text{.} \end{align*}
    The matrix \(\smallmat 0{-1}10\) is a counterclockwise rotation by \(90^\circ\text{.}\) If we rotate by \(90^\circ\) four times, then we end up where we started. Hence rotating by \(90^\circ\) one hundred times is the identity transformation, so
    \begin{align*} A^{100} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} -2 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\inv = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.2 Geometry of Similar Matrices

    Similarity is a very interesting construction when viewed geometrically. We will see that, roughly, similar matrices do the same thing in different coordinate systems. The reader might want to review \(\cB\)-coordinates and nonstandard coordinate grids in Section 2.8 before reading this subsection.
    🔗
    By conditions 4 and 5 of the Theorem 31 of Section 5.1, an \(n\times n\) matrix \(C\) is invertible if and only if its columns \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\) form a basis for \(\R^n\text{.}\) This means we can speak of the \(\cB\)-coordinates of a vector in \(\R^n\text{,}\) where \(\cB\) is the basis of columns of \(C\text{.}\) Recall that
    \begin{align*} [x]_\cB = \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \\ c_n \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{means} x = c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_nv_n = \begin{pmatrix} | \amp | \amp | \\ v_1 \amp v_2 \amp \cdots \amp v_n \\ | \amp | \amp | \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \\ c_n \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Since \(C\) is the matrix with columns \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\text{,}\) this says that \(x = C[x]_\cB\text{.}\) Multiplying both sides by \(C\inv\) gives \([x]_\cB = C\inv x.\) To summarize:
    🔗
    Let \(C\) be an invertible \(n\times n\) matrix with columns \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\text{,}\) and let \(\cB = \{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\}\text{,}\) a basis for \(\R^n\text{.}\) Then for any \(x\) in \(\R^n\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{gather*} C[x]_\cB = x \sptxt{and} C\inv x = [x]_\cB\text{.} \end{gather*}
    This says that \(C\) changes from the \(\cB\)-coordinates to the usual coordinates, and \(C\inv\) changes from the usual coordinates to the \(\cB\)-coordinates.
    🔗
    🔗
    Suppose that \(A = CBC\inv.\) The above observation gives us another way of computing \(Ax\) for a vector \(x\) in \(\R^n\text{.}\) Recall that \(CBC\inv x = C(B(C\inv x)),\) so that multiplying \(CBC\inv\) by \(x\) means first multiplying by \(C\inv\text{,}\) then by \(B\text{,}\) then by \(C\text{.}\) See Example 24 of Section 3.4.
    🔗

    Recipe 5.3.9 Computing \(Ax\) in terms of \(B\).

    Suppose that \(A = CBC\inv,\) where \(C\) is an invertible matrix with columns \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n.\) Let \(\cB = \{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\}\text{,}\) a basis for \(\R^n\text{.}\) Let \(x\) be a vector in \(\R^n\text{.}\) To compute \(Ax\text{,}\) one does the following:
    1. Multiply \(x\) by \(C\inv\text{,}\) which changes to the \(\cB\)-coordinates: \([x]_\cB = C\inv x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Multiply this by \(B\text{:}\) \(B[x]_\cB = BC\inv x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Interpreting this vector as a \(\cB\)-coordinate vector, we multiply it by \(C\) to change back to the usual coordinates: \(Ax = CBC\inv x = CB[x]_\cB.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    
    🔗
    To summarize: if \(A = CBC\inv\text{,}\) then \(A\) and \(B\) do the same thing, only in different coordinate systems.
    🔗
    🔗
    The following example is the heart of this section.
    🔗

    Example 5.3.10.

    Consider the matrices
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 \amp 3/2 \\ 3/2 \amp 1/2 \end{pmatrix} \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix} \qquad C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    One can verify that \(A = CBC\inv\text{:}\) see Example 11 of Section 5.4. Let \(v_1 = {1\choose 1}\) and \(v_2 = {1\choose -1}\text{,}\) the columns of \(C\text{,}\) and let \(\cB = \{v_1,v_2\}\text{,}\) a basis of \(\R^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The matrix \(B\) is diagonal: it scales the \(x\)-direction by a factor of \(2\) and the \(y\)-direction by a factor of \(-1\text{.}\) \def\theo{\includegraphics[width=6cm]{theo11.jpg}} \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=.8, thin border nodes] \node[transform shape] at (0, 0) {\theo}; \draw[seq-violet, opacity=1, vector] (0,0) -- (1,0) node[right] {$e_1$}; \draw[seq-blue, opacity=1, vector] (0,0) -- (0,1) node[above] {$e_2$}; \point at (0, 0); \begin{scope}[xshift=10cm, xscale=2, yscale=-1] \clip (-1.5, -3) rectangle (1.5, 3); \node[transform shape] at (0, 0) {\theo}; \draw[seq-violet, opacity=1, vector] (0,0) -- (1, 0) node[right] {$Be_1$}; \draw[seq-blue, opacity=1, vector] (0,0) -- (0, 1) node[below] {$Be_2$}; \point at (0, 0); \end{scope} \draw (-3, -3) rectangle (3, 3); \draw[xshift=10cm] (-3, -3) rectangle (3, 3); \draw[->, shorten=2mm, thick] (3, 1) to[bend left, "$B$" above=.5mm] (7, 1); \end{tikzpicture} To compute \(Ax\text{,}\) first we multiply by \(C\inv\) to find the \(\cB\)-coordinates of \(x\text{,}\) then we multiply by \(B\text{,}\) then we multiply by \(C\) again. For instance, let \(\textcolor{seq-green}x = {0\choose -2}\text{.}\)
    1. We see from the \(\cB\)-coordinate grid below that \(\textcolor{seq-green}x = -\textcolor{seq-violet}{v_1} + \mhb{v_2}\text{.}\) Therefore, \(C\inv\textcolor{seq-green}x = \mhg{[x]_\cB} = {-1\choose 1}.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Multiplying by \(B\) scales the coordinates: \(\mh{B[x]_\cB} = {-2\choose -1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Interpreting \({-2\choose -1}\) as a \(\cB\)-coordinate vector, we multiply by \(C\) to get
      \begin{gather*} \mh{Ax} = C\begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = -2\textcolor{seq-violet}{v_1} - \mhb{v_2} = \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
      Of course, this vector lies at \((-2, -1)\) on the \(\cB\)-coordinate grid.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    

    Subsection 5.3.3 Eigenvalues of Similar Matrices

    Since similar matrices behave in the same way with respect to different coordinate systems, we should expect their eigenvalues and eigenvectors to be closely related.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.3.18.

    Similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose that \(A = CBC\inv\text{,}\) where \(A,B,C\) are \(n\times n\) matrices. We calculate
    \begin{align*} A - \lambda I_n \amp= CBC\inv - \lambda CC\inv = CBC\inv - C\lambda C\inv\\ \amp= CBC\inv - C\lambda I_n C\inv = C(B-\lambda I_n)C\inv\text{.} \end{align*}
    Therefore,
    \begin{gather*} \det(A-\lambda I_n) = \det(C(B-\lambda I_n)C\inv) = \det(C)\det(B-\lambda I_n)\det(C)\inv = \det(B-\lambda I_n)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Here we have used the Proposition 18 of Section 4.1 and its Corollary 19 of Section 4.1.
    🔗
    🔗
    Since the eigenvalues of a matrix are the roots of its characteristic polynomial, we have shown:
    🔗
    Similar matrices have the same eigenvalues.
    🔗
    🔗
    By Theorem 10 of Section 5.2, similar matrices also have the same trace and determinant.
    🔗

    Note 5.3.19.

    The converse of the Theorem 18 is false. Indeed, the matrices
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{and} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
    both have characteristic polynomial \(f(\lambda) = (\lambda-1)^2\text{,}\) but they are not similar, because the only matrix that is similar to \(I_2\) is \(I_2\) itself.
    🔗
    🔗
    Given that similar matrices have the same eigenvalues, one might guess that they have the same eigenvectors as well. Upon reflection, this is not what one should expect: indeed, the eigenvectors should only match up after changing from one coordinate system to another. This is the content of the next fact, remembering that \(C\) and \(C\inv\) change between the usual coordinates and the \(\cB\)-coordinates.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.3.20.

    Suppose that \(A = CBC\inv\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} v \text{ is an eigenvector of } A \amp\quad\implies\quad C\inv v \text{ is an eigenvector of } B\\ v \text{ is an eigenvector of } B \amp\quad\implies\quad C v \text{ is an eigenvector of } A\text{.} \end{align*}
    The eigenvalues of \(v\) / \(C\inv v\) or \(v\) / \(Cv\) are the same.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose that \(v\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{,}\) so that \(Av = \lambda v\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{gather*} B(C\inv v) = C\inv(CBC\inv v) = C\inv(Av) = C\inv\lambda v = \lambda(C\inv v)\text{,} \end{gather*}
    so that \(C\inv v\) is an eigenvector of \(B\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{.}\) Likewise if \(v\) is an eigenvector of \(B\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{,}\) then \(Bv = \lambda v\text{,}\) and we have
    \begin{gather*} A(Cv) = (CBC\inv)Cv = CBv = C(\lambda v) = \lambda(Cv)\text{,} \end{gather*}
    so that \(Cv\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    If \(A = CBC\inv\text{,}\) then \(C\inv\) takes the \(\lambda\)-eigenspace of \(A\) to the \(\lambda\)-eigenspace of \(B\text{,}\) and \(C\) takes the \(\lambda\)-eigenspace of \(B\) to the \(\lambda\)-eigenspace of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
