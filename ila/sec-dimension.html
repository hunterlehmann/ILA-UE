    Section 2.7 Basis and Dimension

    Objectives

    1. Understand the definition of a basis of a subspace.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Understand the basis theorem.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Recipes: basis for a column space, basis for a null space, basis of a span.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Picture: basis of a subspace of \(\R^2\) or \(\R^3\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Theorem: basis theorem.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Essential vocabulary words: basis, dimension.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.7.1 Basis of a Subspace

    As we discussed in Section 2.6, a subspace is the same as a span, except we do not have a set of spanning vectors in mind. There are infinitely many choices of spanning sets for a nonzero subspace; to avoid reduncancy, usually it is most convenient to choose a spanning set with the minimal number of vectors in it. This is the idea behind the notion of a basis.
    🔗

    Definition 2.7.1.

    Let \(V\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\text{.}\) A basis of \(V\) is a set of vectors \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\) in \(V\) such that:
    1. \(V = \Span\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\text{,}\) and
      🔗
      🔗
    2. the set \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\) is linearly independent.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Recall that a set of vectors is linearly independent if and only if, when you remove any vector from the set, the span shrinks (Theorem 2.5.12). In other words, if \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\) is a basis of a subspace \(V\text{,}\) then no proper subset of \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\) will span \(V\text{:}\) it is a minimal spanning set. Any subspace admits a basis by Theorem 14 of Section 2.6.
    🔗

    Insight 2.7.2.

    A nonzero subspace has infinitely many different bases, but they all contain the same number of vectors.
    🔗
    🔗
    We leave it as an exercise to prove that any two bases have the same number of vectors; one might want to wait until after learning the invertible matrix theorem in [cross-reference to target(s) "sec-matrix-inverses" missing or not unique].
    🔗

    Definition 2.7.3.

    Let \(V\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\text{.}\) The number of vectors in any basis of \(V\) is called the dimension of \(V\text{,}\) and is written \(\dim V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.7.4 A basis of \(\R^2\).

    Find a basis of \(\R^2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    We need to find two vectors in \(\R^2\) that span \(\R^2\) and are linearly independent. One such basis is \(\bigl\{{1\choose 0},{0\choose 1}\bigr\}\text{:}\)
    1. They span because any vector \(a\choose b\) can be written as a linear combination of \({1\choose 0},{0\choose 1}\text{:}\)
      \begin{gather*} \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix} = a\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + b\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. They are linearly independent: if
      \begin{gather*} x\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + y\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \end{gather*}
      then \(x=y=0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    This shows that the plane \(\R^2\) has dimension 2.
    🔗
    
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.7.5 All bases of \(\R^2\).

    Find all bases of \(\R^2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    We know from Example 4 that \(\R^2\) has dimension 2, so any basis of \(\R^2\) has two vectors in it. Let \(v_1,v_2\) be vectors in \(\R^2\text{,}\) and let \(A\) be the matrix with columns \(v_1,v_2\text{.}\)
    1. To say that \(\{v_1,v_2\}\) spans \(\R^2\) means that \(A\) has a pivot in every row: see Theorem 16 of Section 2.3.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. To say that \(\{v_1,v_2\}\) is linearly independent means that \(A\) has a pivot in every column: see Recipe 9 of Section 2.5.
      🔗
      🔗
    Since \(A\) is a \(2\times 2\) matrix, it has a pivot in every row exactly when it has a pivot in every column. Hence any two noncollinear vectors form a basis of \(\R^2\text{.}\) For example,
    \begin{gather*} \left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\right\} \end{gather*}
    is a basis.
    🔗
    
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.7.6 The standard basis of \(\R^n\).

    One shows exactly as in the above Example 4 that the standard coordinate vectors
    \begin{gather*} e_1=\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\quad e_2=\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad\ldots,\quad e_{n-1}=\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\quad e_n=\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \end{gather*}
    form a basis for \(\R^n\text{.}\) This is sometimes known as the standard basis.
    🔗
    In particular, \(\R^n\) has dimension \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.7.7.

    Example 6 implies that any basis for \(\R^n\) has \(n\) vectors in it. Let \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\) be vectors in \(\R^n\text{,}\) and let \(A\) be the \(n\times n\) matrix with columns \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\text{.}\)
    1. To say that \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\}\) spans \(\R^n\) means that \(A\) has a pivot position in every row: see Theorem 16 of Section 2.3.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. To say that \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\}\) is linearly independent means that \(A\) has a pivot position in every column: see Recipe 9 of Section 2.5.
      🔗
      🔗
    Since \(A\) is a square matrix, it has a pivot in every row if and only if it has a pivot in every column. We will see in [cross-reference to target(s) "sec-matrix-inverses" missing or not unique] that the above two conditions are equivalent to the invertibility of the matrix \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.7.8.

    Let
    \begin{gather*} V = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix} \text{ in } \R^3\bigm| x + 3y + z = 0 \right\} \qquad \cB = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} \right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Verify that \(V\) is a subspace, and show directly that \(\cB\) is a basis for \(V\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    First we observe that \(V\) is the solution set of the homogeneous equation \(x + 3y + z = 0\text{,}\) so it is a subspace: see Summary . To show that \(\cB\) is a basis, we really need to verify three things:
    1. Both vectors are in \(V\) because
      \begin{align*} \spalignsysdelims.. \left\{\begin{alignedat}{5} ( \amp {}-{} \amp 3) \amp {}+{} \amp 3(1) \amp {}+{} \amp (0) \amp {}={} \amp 0 \amp {} \\ (0) \amp {}+{} \amp 3(1) \amp {}+{} \amp ( \amp {}-{} \amp 3) \amp {}={} \amp 0. \amp {} \end{alignedat}\right\text{.} \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Span: suppose that \(\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix}\) is in \(V\text{.}\) Since \(x + 3y + z = 0\) we have \(y = -\frac 13(x+z)\text{,}\) so
      \begin{gather*} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ -\frac \\ 13(x+z) \\ z \end{pmatrix} = -\frac x3\begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} - \frac z3\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
      Hence \(\cB\) spans \(V\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Linearly independent:
      \begin{gather*} c_1\begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + c_2\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} = 0 \implies \begin{pmatrix} -3c_1 \\ c_1+c_2 \\ -3c_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \implies c_1 = c_2 = 0\text{.} \end{gather*}
      Alternatively, one can observe that the two vectors are not collinear.
      🔗
      🔗
    Since \(V\) has a basis with two vectors, it has dimension two: it is a plane.
    🔗
    This example is somewhat contrived, in that we will learn systematic methods for verifying that a subset is a basis. The intention is to illustrate the defining properties of a basis.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.7.2 Computing a Basis for a Subspace

    Now we show how to find bases for the column space of a matrix and the null space of a matrix. In order to find a basis for a given subspace, it is usually best to rewrite the subspace as a column space or a null space first: see Summary .
    🔗

    A basis for the column space.

    First we show how to compute a basis for the column space of a matrix.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.7.10.

    The pivot columns of a matrix \(A\) form a basis for \(\Col(A)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    This is a restatement of a Theorem 25 of Section 2.5.
    🔗
    🔗

    Insight 2.7.11.

    The above theorem is referring to the pivot columns in the original matrix, not its reduced row echelon form. Indeed, a matrix and its reduced row echelon form generally have different column spaces. For example, in the matrix \(A\) below:
    🔗
    
    🔗
    the pivot columns are the first two columns, so a basis for \(\Col(A)\) is
    \begin{gather*} \left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    The first two columns of the reduced row echelon form certainly span a different subspace, as
    \begin{gather*} \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\right\} = \left\{\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\Bigm|a, b \text{ in } \R\right\} = \text{($xy$-plane)}\text{,} \end{gather*}
    but \(\Col(A)\) contains vectors whose last coordinate is nonzero.
    🔗
    🔗

    Corollary 2.7.12.

    The dimension of \(\Col(A)\) is the number of pivots of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    A basis of a span.

    Computing a basis for a span is the same as computing a basis for a column space. Indeed, the span of finitely many vectors \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\) is the column space of a matrix, namely, the matrix \(A\) whose columns are \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} | \amp | \amp | \\ v_1 \amp v_2 \amp \cdots \amp v_m \\ | \amp | \amp | \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Example 2.7.13 A basis of a span.

    Find a basis of the subspace
    \begin{gather*} V = \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 5 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The subspace \(V\) is the column space of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \amp -1 \\ -2 \amp -3 \amp 4 \amp 5 \\ 2 \amp 4 \amp 0 \amp -2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The reduced row echelon form of this matrix is
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp -8 \amp -7 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 4 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The first two columns are pivot columns, so a basis for \(V\) is
    \begin{gather*} \left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.7.15 Another basis of the same span.

    Find a basis of the subspace
    \begin{gather*} V = \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 5 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}\right\} \end{gather*}
    which does not consist of the first two vectors, as in Example 13.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The point of this example is that the above Theorem 10 gives one basis for \(V\text{;}\) as always, there are infinitely more.
    🔗
    Reordering the vectors, we can express \(V\) as the column space of
    \begin{align*} A' = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \amp -1 \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 4 \amp 5 \amp -2 \amp -3 \\ 0 \amp -2 \amp 2 \amp 4 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The reduced row echelon form of this matrix is
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 3/4 \amp 7/4 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \amp -2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The first two columns are pivot columns, so a basis for \(V\) is
    \begin{gather*} \left\{\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 5 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    These are the last two vectors in the given spanning set.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    A basis for the null space.

    In order to compute a basis for the null space of a matrix, one has to find the parametric vector form of the solutions of the homogeneous equation \(Ax=0\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 2.7.17.

    The vectors attached to the free variables in the parametric vector form of the solution set of \(Ax=0\) form a basis of \(\Nul(A)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The proof of the theorem has two parts. The first part is that every solution lies in the span of the given vectors. This is automatic: the vectors are exactly chosen so that every solution is a linear combination of those vectors. The second part is that the vectors are linearly independent. This part was discussed in Example 8 of Section 2.5.
    🔗
    🔗

    A basis for a general subspace.

    As mentioned at the beginning of this subsection, when given a subspace written in a different form, in order to compute a basis it is usually best to rewrite it as a column space or null space of a matrix.
    🔗

    Example 2.7.18 A basis of a subspace.

    Let \(V\) be the subspace defined by
    \begin{gather*} V = \left\{\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix}\bigm| x + 2y = z\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Find a basis for \(V\text{.}\) What is \(\dim(V)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First we notice that \(V\) is exactly the solution set of the homogeneous linear equation \(x + 2y - z = 0\text{.}\) Hence \(V = \Nul\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}.\) This matrix is in reduced row echelon form; the parametric form of the general solution is \(x = -2y + z\text{,}\) so the parametric vector form is
    \begin{gather*} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix} = y\begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + z\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    It follows that a basis is
    \begin{gather*} \left\{\begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Since \(V\) has a basis with two vectors, its dimension is \(2\text{:}\) it is a plane.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.7.3 The Basis Theorem

    Recall that \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\}\) forms a basis for \(\R^n\) if and only if the matrix \(A\) with columns \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\) has a pivot in every row and column (see Example 7). Since \(A\) is an \(n\times n\) matrix, these two conditions are equivalent: the vectors span if and only if they are linearly independent. The basis theorem is an abstract version of the preceding statement, that applies to any subspace.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.7.19.

    Let \(V\) be a subspace of dimension \(m\text{.}\) Then:
    • Any \(m\) linearly independent vectors in \(V\) form a basis for \(V\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Any \(m\) vectors that span \(V\) form a basis for \(V\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose that \(\cB = \{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\) is a set of linearly independent vectors in \(V\text{.}\) In order to show that \(\cB\) is a basis for \(V\text{,}\) we must prove that \(V = \Span\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}.\) If not, then there exists some vector \(v_{m+1}\) in \(V\) that is not contained in \(\Span\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}.\) By the Theorem 14 of Section 2.5, the set \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m,v_{m+1}\}\) is also linearly independent. Continuing in this way, we keep choosing vectors until we eventually do have a linearly independent spanning set: say \(V = \Span\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m,\ldots,v_{m+k}\}\text{.}\) Then \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_{m+k}\}\) is a basis for \(V\text{,}\) which implies that \(\dim(V) = m+k > m\text{.}\) But we were assuming that \(V\) has dimension \(m\text{,}\) so \(\cB\) must have already been a basis.
    🔗
    Now suppose that \(\cB = \{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\) spans \(V\text{.}\) If \(\cB\) is not linearly independent, then by Theorem 12 of Section 2.5, we can remove some number of vectors from \(\cB\) without shrinking its span. After reordering, we can assume that we removed the last \(k\) vectors without shrinking the span, and that we cannot remove any more. Now \(V = \Span\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_{m-k}\}\text{,}\) and \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_{m-k}\}\) is a basis for \(V\) because it is linearly independent. This implies that \(\dim V=m-k < m\text{.}\) But we were assuming that \(\dim V = m\text{,}\) so \(\cB\) must have already been a basis.
    🔗
    🔗
    In other words, if you already know that \(\dim V = m\text{,}\) and if you have a set of \(m\) vectors \(\cB = \{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\) in \(V\text{,}\) then you only have to check one of:
    1. \(\cB\) is linearly independent, or
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\cB\) spans \(V\text{,}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    in order for \(\cB\) to be a basis of \(V\text{.}\) If you did not already know that \(\dim V = m\text{,}\) then you would have to check both properties.
    🔗
    To put it yet another way, suppose we have a set of vectors \(\cB = \{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\) in a subspace \(V\text{.}\) Then if any two of the following statements is true, the third must also be true:
    1. \(\cB\) is linearly independent,
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\cB\) spans \(V\text{,}\) and
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \dim V = m.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    For example, if \(V\) is a plane, then any two noncollinear vectors in \(V\) form a basis.
    🔗

    Example 2.7.20 Two noncollinear vectors form a basis of a plane.

    Find a basis of the subspace
    \begin{gather*} V = \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 5 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}\right\} \end{gather*}
    which is different from the bases in Example 13 and Example 15.
    🔗

    Solution.

    We know from the previous examples that \(\dim V = 2\text{.}\) By the Theorem 19, it suffices to find any two noncollinear vectors in \(V\text{.}\) We write two linear combinations of the four given spanning vectors, chosen at random:
    \begin{gather*} w_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -5 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \qquad w_2 = -\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} + \frac 12\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 5 \\ -4 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Since \(w_1,w_2\) are not collinear, \(\cB = \{w_1,w_2\}\) is a basis for \(V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.7.22 Finding a basis by inspection.

    Find a basis for the plane
    \begin{gather*} V = \left\{\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix}\bigm| x_1 + x_2 = x_3\right\} \end{gather*}
    by inspection. (This plane is expressed in set builder notation.)
    🔗

    Solution.

    First note that \(V\) is the null space of the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\text{;}\) this matrix is in reduced row echelon form and has two free variables, so \(V\) is indeed a plane. We write down two vectors satisfying \(x_1 + x_2 = x_3\text{:}\)
    \begin{gather*} v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \qquad v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Since \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) are not collinear, they are linearly independent; since \(\dim(V) = 2\text{,}\) the basis theorem implies that \(\{v_1,v_2\}\) is a basis for \(V\text{.}\)

    True. Since \(S\) is a plane through the origin in \({\bf R}^3\text{,}\) it is a subspace of \({\bf R}^3\) and it is two-dimensional, so any basis for \(S\) conssists of exactly two vectors.

    2.

      If two vectors are in the same plane through the origin in \({\bf R}^3\text{,}\) and if the two vectors are not collinear, then they form a basis for the plane.
      🔗

    • True.

    • False.

    Solution.
    True. The span of the two vectors must be a plane, otherwise their span would be a line or a point which is impossible since the two vectors is not collinear. Any two vectors that span a plane must be linearly independent, therefore the two vectors form a basis for the plane.

      The dimension of the null space of \(A\) is the number of pivots of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗

    • True.

    • False.

    Solution.
    False: \(\dim(\Nul \ A)\) is the number of columns in \(A\) that do NOT have a pivot.

    4.

      If \(b\) lies in the column space of \(A\) and the columns of \(A\) are linearly independent, then \(Ax = b\) has infinitely many solutions.
      🔗

    • True.

    • False.

    Solution.
    False: the columns of \(A\) are linearly independent, so \(A\) has a pivot in every column, therefore the augmented matrix \((A | b)\) has a pivot in each column to the left of the augment bar, so either the system is inconsistent or has exactly one solution. We were told that \(b\) is in the column space of \(A\text{,}\) so the system is consistent, hence \(Ax = b\) has exactly one solution.

    5.

      Any three vectors that span \({\bf R}^3\) must be linearly independent.
      🔗

    • True.

    • False.

    Solution.
    True. Let \(A\) be the \(3 \times 3\) matrix whose columns are the three vectors. Since these vectors span \({\bf R}^3\text{,}\) we conclude \(A\) has a pivot in every row, so \(A\) has \(3\) pivots. This means that \(A\) has a pivot in each of its \(3\) columns, so its columns are linearly independent.

    6.

    How many vectors are there in a basis for a line \(S\) through the origin in \({\bf R}^7\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    One. Take any nonzero vector \(v\) along the line \(S\text{.}\) Then \(\{v\}\) is linearly independent, and any vector along the line \(S\) can be obtained by scaling \(v\) by an appropriate scalar \(c\text{.}\) In fact, the set of all vectors of the form \(cv\) (where \(c\) is a scalar) is the line \(S\text{.}\) Therefore, \(\{v\}\) is a basis for \(S\text{.}\)

    7.

    Find a basis for the \(yz\)-plane in \({\bf R}^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The \(yz\)-plane is all vectors of the form \(\begin{pmatrix}0 \\ y \\ z\end{pmatrix}\) where \(y\) and \(z\) are real. A basis for the \(yz\)-plane is \(\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{:}\) the two vectors are clearly linearly independent, and they span the \(yz\)-plane since any vector of the form \(\begin{pmatrix}0 \\ y \\ z\end{pmatrix}\) can be written as the linear combination
    \begin{gather*} \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ y \\ z\end{pmatrix} = y \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix} + z \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}

    8.

    Find a basis for the column space and the null space of
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix}1 \amp 2 \amp 3 \\ 4 \amp 5 \amp 6 \\ -7 \amp -8 \amp -9\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    We row-reduce \(\left(\begin{array}{r|r}A \amp 0\end{array}\right)\) to find
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r}1 \amp 2 \amp 3 \amp 0 \\ 4 \amp 5 \amp 6 \amp 0 \\ -7 \amp -8 \amp -9 \amp 0\end{array}\right) \xrightarrow[]{RREF} \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r}1 \amp 0 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0\end{array}\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
    The pivot columns of \(A\) form a basis for \(\Col(A)\text{:}\)
    \begin{gather*} \left\{\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 4 \\ -7\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 5 \\ -8\end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    For \(\Nul(A)\text{,}\) the RREF of \(\left(\begin{array}{r|r}A \amp 0\end{array}\right)\) gives us \(x_1 - x_3 = 0\text{,}\) \(x_2 + 2x_3 = 0\text{,}\) and \(x_3\) is free, so \(x_1 = x_3\) and \(x_2 = -2x_3\text{.}\)
    \begin{gather*} \begin{pmatrix}x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}x_3 \\ -2x_3 \\ x_3\end{pmatrix} = x_3 \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ -2 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}. \qquad \textup{Basis for } \Nul(A): \left\{\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ -2 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\right\} \end{gather*}

    14.

    Let
    \begin{gather*} V = \left\{\begin{pmatrix}x \\ y \\ z\end{pmatrix} \textup{ in } {\bf R}^3 \ : \ x = y \right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗

    (a)

    Show that \(V\) is a subspace of \({\bf R}^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We could verify that \(V\) is a subspace using the properties of a subspace, but there is a much faster way. \(V\) is the set of all points \((x, y, z)\) in \({\bf R}^3\) so that \(x - y = 0\text{.}\) This is \(\textup{Nul}\begin{pmatrix}1 \amp -1 \amp 0\end{pmatrix}\text{,}\) which is automatically a subspace of \({\bf R}^3\) since the null space of \textit{any} matrix with \(3\) columns is a subspace of \({\bf R}^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find a basis for \(V\text{.}\) (Hint: it may be helpful to write \(V\) as the null space of some matrix)
    🔗
    Solution.
    To find \(\textup{Nul}\begin{pmatrix}1 \amp -1 \amp 0\end{pmatrix}\) we use the augmented matrix \(\left(\begin{array}{rrr|r}1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \amp 0\end{array}\right)\text{,}\) which gives us \(x_1 = x_2\text{,}\) \(x_2\) is free, and \(x_3\) is free. Therefore, a basis for \(V\) is
    \begin{gather*} \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}

We just need to write a matrix \(B\) so that \(\Col(B)= \textup{Span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\right\} = V\text{.}\) One way to do this is just to let
    \begin{align*} B = \begin{pmatrix}1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}

    15.

    (Challenge) Consider the set \(W\) consisting of all polynomials that have degree \(2\) or less. In other words, \(W\) is the set of all functions of the form
    \begin{gather*} f(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 \end{gather*}
    where \(a_0\text{,}\) \(a_1\text{,}\) and \(a_2\) are real numbers.
    🔗

    (a)

    What should it mean for a set of polynomials in \(W\) to be linearly independent?
    🔗
    Solution.
    A set of polynomials \(\{f_1, \ldots, f_k\}\) in \(W\) is linearly independent if the equation
    \begin{gather*} c_1 f_1 + \cdots + c_k f_k = {\bf 0} \end{gather*}
    has only the trivial solution \(c_1 = \cdots c_k = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In other words, if
    \begin{gather*} c_1 f_1(x) + \cdots + c_k f_k(x) = 0 \end{gather*}
    for every real number \(x\text{,}\) then \(c_1 = \cdots c_k = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find a basis for \(W\text{.}\) In other words, find a linearly independent set \(S\) of polynomials in \(W\text{,}\) so that any polynomial in \(W\) can be written as a linear combination of elements in \(S\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    A basis for \(W\) is \(\{f_1, f_2, f_3\}\) where
    \begin{gather*} f_1(x) = 1 + 0x + 0x^2, \qquad f_2(x) = 0 + 1x + 0x^2, \qquad f_3(x) = 0 + 0x + 1x^2\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    First we show that \(f_1, f_2\text{,}\) and \(f_3\) span \(W\text{.}\) Let \(f\) be any polynomial in \(W\text{.}\) Then for some \(a_0\text{,}\) \(a_1\text{,}\) and \(a_2\) we have
    \begin{gather*} f(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2\text{,} \end{gather*}
    so \(f= a_0 f_1 + a_1 f_2 + a_2 f_3\) and therefore \(f\) is in \(\textup{Span}\{f_1, f_2, f_3\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Also, \(\{f_1, f_2, f_3\}\) is linearly independent: if
    \begin{gather*} {\bf 0} = c_1 f_1 + c_2 f_2 + c_3 f_3\text{,} \end{gather*}
    then for all \(x\) we have
    \begin{gather*} 0 = c_1 + c_2 x + c_3 x^2\text{.} \end{gather*}
    This means that the polynomial \(c_1 + c_2 x + c_3 x^2\) has infinitely many roots, which is only possible if all coefficients are \(0\) (otherwise, it would have a maximum of \(2\) roots).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    What is the dimension of \(W\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since \(W\) has a basis consisting of \(3\) elements, the dimension of \(W\) is \(3\text{.}\)
