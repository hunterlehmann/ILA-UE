    Section 2.8 Bases as Coordinate Systems

    Objectives

    1. Learn to view a basis as a coordinate system on a subspace.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Recipes: compute the \(\cB\)-coordinates of a vector, compute the usual coordinates of a vector from its \(\cB\)-coordinates.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Picture: the \(\cB\)-coordinates of a vector using its location on a nonstandard coordinate grid.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Vocabulary word: \(\cB\)-coordinates.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    In this section, we interpret a basis of a subspace \(V\) as a coordinate system on \(V\text{,}\) and we learn how to write a vector in \(V\) in that coordinate system.
    🔗

    Fact 2.8.1.

    If \(\cB = \{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\) is a basis for a subspace \(V\text{,}\) then any vector \(x\) in \(V\) can be written as a linear combination
    \begin{gather*} x = c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_mv_m \end{gather*}
    in exactly one way.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Recall that to say \(\cB\) is a basis for \(V\) means that \(\cB\) spans \(V\) and \(\cB\) is linearly independent. Since \(\cB\) spans \(V\text{,}\) we can write any \(x\) in \(V\) as a linear combination of \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\text{.}\) For uniqueness, suppose that we had two such expressions:
    \begin{align*} x \amp= c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_mv_m\\ x \amp= c_1'v_1 + c_2'v_2 + \cdots + c_m'v_m\text{.} \end{align*}
    Subtracting the first equation from the second yields
    \begin{gather*} 0 = x-x = (c_1-c_1')v_1 + (c_2-c_2')v_2 + \cdots + (c_m-c_m')v_m\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Since \(\cB\) is linearly independent, the only solution to the above equation is the trivial solution: all the coefficients must be zero. It follows that \(c_i-c_i'\) for all \(i\text{,}\) which proves that \(c_1=c_1',\,c_2=c_2',\ldots,\,c_m=c_m'\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.8.2.

    Consider the standard basis of \(\R^3\) from Example 6 of Section 2.7:
    \begin{gather*} e_1=\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\quad e_2=\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad e_3=\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    According to the above Fact 1, every vector in \(\R^3\) can be written as a linear combination of \(e_1,e_2,e_3\text{,}\) with unique coefficients. For example,
    \begin{gather*} v = \begin{pmatrix} \color{seq1}3 \\ \color{seq2}5 \\ \color{seq3}{-2} \end{pmatrix} = \textcolor{seq1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \textcolor{seq2}{5}\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \textcolor{seq3}{{}- 2}\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \textcolor{seq1}{3}e_1 + \textcolor{seq2}{5}e_2 \textcolor{seq3}{{}- 2}e_3\text{.} \end{gather*}
    In this case, the coordinates of \(v\) are exactly the coefficients of \(e_1,e_2,e_3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    What exactly are coordinates, anyway? One way to think of coordinates is that they give directions for how to get to a certain point from the origin. In the above example, the linear combination \(3e_1+5e_2-2e_3\) can be thought of as the following list of instructions: start at the origin, travel \(3\) units north, then travel \(5\) units east, then \(2\) units down.
    🔗

    Definition 2.8.3.

    Let \(\cB=\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\) be a basis of a subspace \(V\text{,}\) and let
    \begin{gather*} x = c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_mv_m \end{gather*}
    be a vector in \(V\text{.}\) The coefficients \(c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_m\) are the coordinates of \(x\) with respect to \(\cB\). The \(\cB\)-coordinate vector of \(x\) is the vector
    \begin{gather*} [x]_\cB = \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \\ c_m \end{pmatrix} \quad\text{in }\R^m\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    If we change the basis, then we can still give instructions for how to get to the point \((3,5,-2)\text{,}\) but the instructions will be different. Say for example we take the basis
    \begin{gather*} v_1=e_1+e_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad v_2=e_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\quad v_3 =e_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    We can write \((3,5,-2)\) in this basis as \(3v_1+2v_2-2v_3\text{.}\) In other words: start at the origin, travel northeast \(3\) times as far as \(v_1\text{,}\) then \(2\) units east, then \(2\) units down. In this situation, we can say that “\(3\) is the \(v_1\)-coordinate of \((3,5,-2)\text{,}\) \(2\) is the \(v_2\)-coordinate of \((3,5,-2)\text{,}\) and \(-2\) is the \(v_3\)-coordinate of \((3,5,-2)\text{.}\)”
    🔗
    The above Definition 3 gives a way of using \(\R^m\) to label the points of a subspace of dimension \(m\text{:}\) a point is simply labeled by its \(\cB\)-coordinate vector. For instance, if we choose a basis for a plane, we can label the points of that plane with the points of \(\R^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.8.4 A nonstandard coordinate system on \(\R^2\).

    Define
    \begin{gather*} v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},\; v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix},\quad \cB = \{v_1,\,v_2\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    1. Verify that \(\cB\) is a basis for \(\R^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \([w]_\cB = {1\choose 2}\text{,}\) then what is \(w\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find the \(\cB\)-coordinates of \(v = {5\choose 3}.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Solution.

    1. By the Theorem 19 of Section 2.7, any two linearly independent vectors form a basis for \(\R^2\text{.}\) Clearly \(v_1,v_2\) are not multiples of each other, so they are linearly independent.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. To say \([w]_\cB={1\choose 2}\) means that \(1\) is the \(v_1\)-coordinate of \(w\text{,}\) and that \(2\) is the \(v_2\)-coordinate:
      \begin{gather*} w = v_1 + 2v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + 2\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. We have to solve the vector equation \(v=c_1v_1 + c_2v_2\) in the unknowns \(c_1,c_2\text{.}\) We form an augmented matrix and row reduce:
      \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 \amp 1 \amp 5 \\ 1 \amp -1 \amp 3 \end{array}\right) \quad\xrightarrow{\text{RREF}}\quad \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 \amp 0 \amp 4 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \end{array}\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
      We have \(c_1=4\) and \(c_2=1\text{,}\) so \(v = 4v_1+v_2\) and \([v]_\cB = {4\choose1}.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    In the following picture, we indicate the coordinate system defined by \(\cB\) by drawing lines parallel to the “\(v_1\)-axis” and “\(v_2\)-axis”. Using this grid it is easy to see that the \(\cB\)-coordinates of \(v\) are \({5\choose 1}\text{,}\) and that the \(\cB\)-coordinates of \(w\) are \({1\choose 2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    🔗
    This picture could be the grid of streets in Palo Alto, California. Residents of Palo Alto refer to northwest as “north” and to northeast as “east”. There is a reason for this: the old road to San Francisco is called El Camino Real, and that road runs from the southeast to the northwest in Palo Alto. So when a Palo Alto resident says “go south two blocks and east one block”, they are giving directions from the origin to the Whole Foods at \(w\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.8.6.

    Let
    \begin{gather*} v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \quad v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    These form a basis \(\cB\) for a plane \(V = \Span\{v_1,v_2\}\) in \(\R^3\text{.}\) We indicate the coordinate system defined by \(\cB\) by drawing lines parallel to the “\(v_1\)-axis” and “\(v_2\)-axis”:
    🔗
    
    🔗
    We can see from the picture that the \(v_1\)-coordinate of \(\mh{u_1}\) is equal to \(1\text{,}\) as is the \(v_2\)-coordinate, so \([\textcolor{seq1}{u_1}]_\cB = {1\choose 1}.\) Similarly, we have
    \begin{gather*} [\textcolor{seq2}{u_2}]_\cB = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ \frac 12 \end{pmatrix}\qquad [\textcolor{seq3}{u_3}]_\cB = \begin{pmatrix} \frac 32 \\ -\frac 12 \end{pmatrix}\qquad [\textcolor{seq5}{u_4}]_\cB = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ \frac 32 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.8.8 A coordinate system on a plane.

    Define
    \begin{gather*} v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},\; v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},\quad \cB = \{v_1,\,v_2\}, \quad V = \Span\{v_1,\,v_2\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    1. Verify that \(\cB\) is a basis for \(V\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \([w]_\cB = {5\choose 2}\text{,}\) then what is \(w\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find the \(\cB\)-coordinates of \(v = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Solution.

    1. We need to verify that \(\cB\) spans \(V\text{,}\) and that it is linearly independent. By definition, \(V\) is the span of \(\cB\text{;}\) since \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) are not multiples of each other, they are linearly independent. This shows in particular that \(V\) is a plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. To say \([w]_\cB={5\choose 2}\) means that \(5\) is the \(v_1\)-coordinate of \(w\text{,}\) and that \(2\) is the \(v_2\)-coordinate:
      \begin{gather*} w = 5v_1 + 2v_2 = 5\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + 2\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 2 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. We have to solve the vector equation \(v=c_1v_1 + c_2v_2\) in the unknowns \(c_1,c_2\text{.}\) We form an augmented matrix and row reduce:
      \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 \amp 1 \amp 5 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 5 \end{array}\right) \quad\xrightarrow{\text{RREF}}\quad \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 \amp 0 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{array}\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
      We have \(c_1=2\) and \(c_2=3\text{,}\) so \(v = 2v_1+3v_2\) and \([v]_\cB = {2\choose 3}.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.8.10 A coordinate system on another plane.

    Define
    \begin{gather*} v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix},\; v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},\; v_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 8 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix},\quad V = \Span\{v_1,\,v_2,\,v_3\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    1. Find a basis \(\cB\) for \(V\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the \(\cB\)-coordinates of \(x = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 11 \\ 8 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Solution.

    1. We write \(V\) as the column space of a matrix \(A\text{,}\) then row reduce to find the pivot columns, as in Example 13 of Section 2.7.
      \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 1 \amp 8 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp 6 \end{pmatrix} \quad\xrightarrow{\text{RREF}}\quad \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
      The first two columns are pivot columns, so we can take \(\cB=\{v_1,v_2\}\) as our basis for \(V\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. We have to solve the vector equation \(x = c_1v_1 + c_2v_2\text{.}\) We form an augmented matrix and row reduce:
      \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 2 \amp -1 \amp 4 \\ 3 \amp 1 \amp 11 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp 8 \end{array}\right) \quad\xrightarrow{\text{RREF}}\quad \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 \amp 0 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{array}\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
      We have \(c_1 = 3\) and \(c_2 = 2\text{,}\) so \(x=3v_1 + 2v_2\text{,}\) and thus \([x]_\cB = {3\choose 2}.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Recipes: \(\cB\)-coordinates.

    If \(\cB = \{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\) is a basis for a subspace \(V\) and \(x\) is in \(V\text{,}\) then
    \begin{gather*} [x]_\cB = \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \\ c_m \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{means} x = c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_mv_m\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Finding the \(\cB\)-coordinates of \(x\) means solving the vector equation
    \begin{gather*} x = c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_mv_m \end{gather*}
    in the unknowns \(c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_m\text{.}\) This generally means row reducing the augmented matrix
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} | \amp | \amp | \amp | \\ v_1 \amp v_2 \amp \cdots \amp v_m \amp x \\ | \amp | \amp | \amp | \end{array}\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.8.12.

    Let \(\cB = \{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\) be a basis of a subspace \(V\text{.}\) Finding the \(\cB\)-coordinates of a vector \(x\) means solving the vector equation
    \begin{gather*} x = c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_mv_m\text{.} \end{gather*}
    If \(x\) is not in \(V\text{,}\) then this equation has no solution, as \(x\) is not in \(V = \Span\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\text{.}\) In other words, the above equation is inconsistent when \(x\) is not in \(V\text{.}\)
