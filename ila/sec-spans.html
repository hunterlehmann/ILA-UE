    Section 2.2 Vector Equations and Spans

    Objectives

    1. Understand the equivalence between a system of linear equations and a vector equation.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Learn the definition of \(\Span\{x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_k\}\text{,}\) and how to draw pictures of spans.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Recipe: solve a vector equation using augmented matrices / decide if a vector is in a span.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Pictures: an inconsistent system of equations, a consistent system of equations, spans in \(\R^2\) and \(\R^3\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Vocabulary word: vector equation.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Essential vocabulary word: span.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.1 Vector Equations

    An equation involving vectors with \(n\) coordinates is the same as \(n\) equations involving only numbers. For example, the equation
    \begin{gather*} x\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} + y \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 16 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \end{gather*}
    simplifies to
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ 2x \\ 6x \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} -y \\ -2y \\ -y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 16 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{or} \left(\begin{alignedat}{2} x \amp {}-{} \amp y \amp {} \\ 2x \amp {}-{} \amp 2y \amp {} \\ 6x \amp {}-{} \amp y \amp {} \end{alignedat}\right) = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 16 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    For two vectors to be equal, all of their coordinates must be equal, so this is just the system of linear equations
    \begin{align*} \left\{\begin{alignedat}{3} x \amp {}-{} \amp y \amp {}={} \amp 8 \amp {} \\ 2x \amp {}-{} \amp 2y \amp {}={} \amp 16 \amp {} \\ 6x \amp {}-{} \amp y \amp {}={} \amp 3 \amp . \end{alignedat}\right. \end{align*}
    🔗

    Definition 2.2.1.

    A vector equation is an equation involving a linear combination of vectors with possibly unknown coefficients.
    🔗
    🔗
    Asking whether or not a vector equation has a solution is the same as asking if a given vector is a linear combination of some other given vectors.
    🔗
    🔗
    For example the vector equation above is asking if the vector \((8,16,3)\) is a linear combination of the vectors \((1,2,6)\) and \((-1,2,-1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The thing we really care about is solving systems of linear equations, not solving vector equations. The whole point of vector equations is that they give us a different, and more geometric, way of viewing systems of linear equations.
    🔗

    Note 2.2.2 A Picture of a Consistent System.

    Below we will show that the above system of equations is consistent. Equivalently, this means that the above vector equation has a solution. In other words, there is a linear combination of \((1,2,6)\) and \((-1,2,-1)\) that equals \((8,16,3)\text{.}\) We can visualize the last statement geometrically. Therefore, the following Figure 3 gives a picture of a consistent system of equations. Compare with Figure 9 below, which shows a picture of an inconsistent system.
    🔗
    🔗
    In order to actually solve the vector equation
    \begin{gather*} x\mh{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}} + y \mhg{\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}} = \mhp{\begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 16 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}}\text{,} \end{gather*}
    one has to solve the system of linear equations
    \begin{align*} \left\{\begin{alignedat}{3} x \amp {}-{} \amp y \amp {}={} \amp 8 \amp {} \\ 2x \amp {}-{} \amp 2y \amp {}={} \amp 16 \amp {} \\ 6x \amp {}-{} \amp y \amp {}={} \amp 3 \amp . \end{alignedat}\right. \end{align*}
    This means forming the augmented matrix
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} \mh{1} \amp \mhg{-1} \amp \mhp{8} \\ \mh{2} \amp \mhg{-2} \amp \mhp{16} \\ \mh{6} \amp \mhg{-1} \amp \mhp{3} \end{array}\right) \end{align*}
    and row reducing. Note that the columns of the augmented matrix are the vectors from the original vector equation, so it is not actually necessary to write the system of equations: one can go directly from the vector equation to the augmented matrix by “smooshing the vectors together”. In the following Example 4 we carry out the row reduction and find the solution.
    🔗

    Example 2.2.4.

    Is \(\begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 16 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\) a linear combination of \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\text{?}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    As discussed above, this question boils down to a row reduction:
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 \amp -1 \amp 8 \\ 2 \amp -2 \amp 16 \\ 6 \amp -1 \amp 3 \end{array}\right) \quad\xrightarrow{\text{RREF}}\quad \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 \amp 0 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -9 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{array}\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
    From this we see that the equation is consistent, and the solution is \(x=-1\) and \(y=-9\text{.}\) We conclude that \(\begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 16 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\) is indeed a linear combination of \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\text{,}\) with coefficients \(-1\) and \(-9\text{:}\)
    \begin{gather*} -\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} -9\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 16 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Recipe 2.2.5 Solving a vector equation.

    In general, the vector equation
    \begin{gather*} x_1v_1 + x_2v_2 + \cdots + x_kv_k = b \end{gather*}
    where \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k,\,b\) are vectors in \(\R^n\) and \(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_k\) are unknown scalars, has the same solution set as the linear system with augmented matrix
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} | \amp | \amp {} \amp | \amp | \\ v_1 \amp v_2 \amp \cdots \amp v_k \amp b \\ | \amp | \amp {} \amp | \amp | \end{array}\right) \end{align*}
    whose columns are the \(v_i\)’s and the \(b\)’s.
    🔗
    🔗
    Now we have three equivalent ways of thinking about a linear system:
    1. As a system of equations:
      \begin{align*} \left\{\begin{alignedat}{4} 2x_1 \amp {}+{} \amp 3x_2 \amp {}-{} \amp 2x_3 \amp {}={} \amp 7 \amp {} \\ x_1 \amp {}-{} \amp x_2 \amp {}-{} \amp 3x_3 \amp {}={} \amp 5 \amp {} \end{alignedat}\right. \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. As an augmented matrix:
      \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 2 \amp 3 \amp -2 \amp 7 \\ 1 \amp -1 \amp -3 \amp 5 \end{array}\right) \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. As a vector equation (\(x_1v_1 + x_2v_2 + \cdots + x_nv_n = b\)):
      \begin{gather*} x_1\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + x_2\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} + x_3\begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix} \end{gather*}
      🔗
      🔗
    The third is geometric in nature: it lends itself to drawing pictures.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.2 Spans

    It will be important to know what are all linear combinations of a set of vectors \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k\) in \(\R^n\text{.}\) In other words, we would like to understand the set of all vectors \(b\) in \(\R^n\) such that the vector equation (in the unknowns \(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_k\))
    \begin{gather*} x_1v_1 + x_2v_2 + \cdots + x_kv_k = b \end{gather*}
    has a solution (i.e. is consistent).
    🔗

    Definition 2.2.6.

    Let \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k\) be vectors in \(\R^n\text{.}\) The span of \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k\) is the collection of all linear combinations of \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k\text{,}\) and is denoted \(\Span\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k\}\text{.}\) In symbols:
    \begin{gather*} \Span\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k\} = \bigl\{x_1v_1 + x_2v_2 + \cdots + x_kv_k \mid x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_k \text{ in }\R\bigr\} \end{gather*}
    We also say that \(\Span\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k\}\) is the subset spanned by or generated by the vectors \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The above Definition 6 is the first of several essential definitions that we will see in this textbook. They are essential in that they form the essence of the subject of linear algebra: learning linear algebra means (in part) learning these definitions. All of the definitions are important, but it is essential that you learn and understand the definitions marked as such.
    🔗

    Note 2.2.7 Set Builder Notation.

    The notation
    \begin{gather*} \bigl\{x_1v_1 + x_2v_2 + \cdots + x_kv_k \mid x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_k \text{ in }\R\bigr\} \end{gather*}
    reads as: “the set of all things of the form \(x_1v_1 + x_2v_2 + \cdots + x_kv_k\) such that \(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_k\) are in \(\R\text{.}\)” The vertical line is “such that”; everything to the left of it is “the set of all things of this form”, and everything to the right is the condition that those things must satisfy to be in the set. Specifying a set in this way is called set builder notation.
    🔗
    All mathematical notation is only shorthand: any sequence of symbols must translate into a usual sentence.
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 2.2.8 Three characterizations of consistency.

    Now we have three equivalent ways of making the same statement:
    1. A vector \(b\) is in the span of \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The vector equation
      \begin{gather*} x_1v_1 + x_2v_2 + \cdots + x_kv_k = b \end{gather*}
      has a solution.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The linear system with augmented matrix
      \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} | \amp | \amp {} \amp | \amp | \\ v_1 \amp v_2 \amp \cdots \amp v_k \amp b \\ | \amp | \amp {} \amp | \amp | \end{array}\right) \end{align*}
      is consistent.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Equivalent means that, for any given list of vectors \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k,\,b\text{,}\) either all three statements are true, or all three statements are false.
    🔗

    Pictures of Spans.

    Drawing a picture of \(\Span\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k\}\) is the same as drawing a picture of all linear combinations of \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_k\text{.}\)

    Exercises 2.2.3 Exercises

    1.

      For any collection of vectors \(v_1, \ldots, v_k\) in \({\bf R}^n\text{,}\) the zero vector in \({\bf R}^n\) is a linear combination of \(v_1, \ldots, v_k\text{.}\)
      🔗

    • True.

    • False.

    Solution.
    True: \(0 = 0 v_1 + \cdots + 0 v_k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

      The vector \(\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\) can be written as a linear combination of \(\begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix}-2 \\ -2\end{pmatrix}\) in infinitely many ways.
      🔗

    • True.

    • False.

    Solution.
    True: the equation \(x_1 \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 2\end{pmatrix} + x_2 \begin{pmatrix}-2 \\ -2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\) corresponds to the augmented matrix \(\left(\begin{array}{rr|r}2 \amp -2 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp -2 \amp 1\end{array}\right)\text{.}\) One step of row-reduction gives us \(\left(\begin{array}{rr|r}2 \amp -2 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0\end{array}\right)\text{,}\) so the system is consistent and has a free variable, so it has infinitely many solutions.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

      The vector \(\begin{pmatrix}8 \\ 16 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\) in the span of \(\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 2 \\ 6\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix}-1 \\ -2 \\ -1\end{pmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗

    • True.

    • False.

    Solution.
    True: row reduction quickly gives \(\left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp -1 \amp 8 \\ 2 \amp -2 \amp 16 \\ 6 \amp -1 \amp 1\end{array}\right) \to \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp -1 \amp 8 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 5 \amp -47\end{array}\right)\text{.}\) We don’t need to keep going to RREF to see that this augmented matrix has a pivot in every column except the rightmost column, so it is consistent.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

      If \(v_1, \ldots, v_k\) are vectors in \({\bf R}^n\text{,}\) then the equation \(x_1 v_1 + \cdots + x_k v_k = 0\) must be consistent.
      🔗

    • True.

    • False.

    Solution.
    True: If we set \(x_1\) through \(x_k\) all equal to \(0\text{,}\) then \(0v_1 + \cdots + 0v_k = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

      The plane \(z=1\) in \({\bf R}^3\) is a span.
      🔗

    • True.

    • False.

    Solution.
    False: any span contains the zero vector but the plane \(z=1\) does not contain the zero vector, so it is not a span.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

      The three vectors \(v_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \ v_2 = \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix},\) and \(v_3 = \begin{pmatrix}-1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\) span \({\bf R}^3\text{.}\)
      🔗

    • True.

    • False.

    Solution.
    True. If \(b\) is any vector in \({\bf R}^3\text{,}\) then the vector equation \(x_1 v_1 + x_2 v_2 + x_3 v_3 = b\) corresponds to the augmented matrix
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r}1 \amp 1 \amp -1 \amp \vert \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp b \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp \vert\end{array}\right) \xrightarrow[]{RREF} \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r}1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp * \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp * \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp *\end{array}\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
    From the RREF we see that the augmented matrix cannot have a pivot in the rightmost column, so the system is consistent no matter what \(b\) is. In other words, every \(b\) in \({\bf R}^3\) is in \(\Span\{v_1, v_2, v_3\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    7.

      If \(u\text{,}\) \(v\text{,}\) and \(w\) are nonzero vectors in \({\bf R}^2\text{,}\) then Span\(\{u, v, w\}\) is \({\bf R}^2\text{.}\)
      🔗

    • True.

    • False.

    Solution.
    False. Take \(v_1=v_2=v_3=\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) Many other counterexamples possible.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8.

      The line \(y = 2x + 1\) is a span of some number of vectors in \({\bf R}^2\text{.}\)
      🔗

    • True.

    • False.

    Solution.
    False. The span of any number of vectors must include the origin, but the line \(y = 2x + 1\) does not go through the origin.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    9.

    Suppose \(v\) is a vector in \({\bf R}^n\text{,}\) and consider the set of all linear combinations of \(v\text{.}\) What geometric shape(s) can this be?
    🔗
    Solution.
    If \(v\) is the zero vector, then the set of all linear combinations of \(v\) is just the zero vector, since \(c(0)=0\) no matter what the scalar \(c\) is.
    🔗
    If \(v\) is not the zero vector, then the set of all linear combinations of \(v\) is a line. More specifically, it is the line through the origin and \(v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    10.

    Consider the following two vectors which characterize two colors in terms of Red, Green, and Blue.
    \begin{gather*} \begin{pmatrix}180 \\ 50 \\ 200\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}100 \\ 150 \\ 100\end{pmatrix} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    
    🔗
    For which \(h\) can we get \(\begin{pmatrix}116 \\ 130 \\ h\end{pmatrix}\) as a combination of those two colors?
    🔗
    
    🔗
    Solution.
    We put everything into an augmented matrix and row reduce.
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}180 \amp 100 \amp 116\\ 50 \amp 150 \amp 130\\ 200 \amp 100 \amp h\end{array}\right) \amp \xrightarrow[\textup{then } R_1 = \frac{R_1}{50}]{R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 3 \amp 13/5\\ 180 \amp 100 \amp 116\\ 200 \amp 100 \amp h\end{array}\right) \xrightarrow[R_3 = R_3 - 200 R_1]{R_2 = R_2 - 180 R_1} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 3 \amp 13/5\\ 0 \amp -440 \amp -352\\ 0 \amp -500 \amp h-520\end{array}\right)\\ \amp \xrightarrow[]{R_3 = R_3 - (500/440)R_2} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 3 \amp 13/5\\ 0 \amp -440 \amp -352\\ 0 \amp 0 \amp h-120\end{array}\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
    This system is consistent if and only if \(h = 120\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    11.

    Consider the system of equations given by
    \begin{gather*} 5x_1 - x_2 + 5x_3 = 2 \end{gather*}
    \begin{gather*} -x_1 - 8x_3 = 4 \end{gather*}
    \begin{gather*} 2x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 0\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗

    (a)

    Write the system of equations as a vector equation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(x_1 \begin{pmatrix}5 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{pmatrix} + x_2 \begin{pmatrix}-1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} + x_3 \begin{pmatrix}5 \\ -8 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 4 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Solve the vector equation, and use your solution to write the target vector in your equation as a linear combination of the other vectors.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We row-reduce:
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r}5 \amp -1 \amp 5 \amp 2 \\ -1 \amp 0 \amp -8 \amp 4 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp 1 \amp 0\end{array}\right) \xrightarrow[]{RREF} \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r}1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 4/5 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp -3/5\end{array}\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
    This gives \(x_1 = 4/5\text{,}\) \(x_2 = -1\text{,}\) and \(x_3 = -3/5\text{.}\) Therefore,
    \begin{gather*} \frac{4}{5} \begin{pmatrix}5 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}-1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} - \frac{3}{5} \begin{pmatrix}5 \\ -8 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 4 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}

    14.

    Find all solutions to the vector equation
    \begin{gather*} x_1 \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ -4 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} + x_2 \begin{pmatrix}4 \\ 1 \\ -2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 14 \\ -7\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    If there are no solutions, justify why the vector equation is inconsistent.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}2 \amp 4 \amp 2\\ -4 \amp 1 \amp 14\\ 1 \amp -2 \amp -7\end{array}\right) \amp \xrightarrow[]{R_1=R_1/2} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 2 \amp 1\\ -4 \amp 1 \amp 14\\ 1 \amp -2 \amp -7\end{array}\right) \xrightarrow[R_3=R_3-R_1]{R_2=R_2+4R_1} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 2 \amp 1\\ 0 \amp 9 \amp 18\\ 0 \amp -4 \amp -8\end{array}\right)\\ \\ \amp \xrightarrow[R_3=R_3/-4]{R_2=R_2/9} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 2 \amp 1\\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2\\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2\end{array}\right) \xrightarrow[R_1=R_1-2R_2]{R_3=R_3-R_2} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 0 \amp -3\\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2\\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0\end{array}\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
    Therefore, \(x_1= \ -3\) \ \ and \ \(x_2=2\text{.}\)

    16.

    Find all values of \(b\) (if there are any) so that \(\begin{pmatrix}b \\ -3 \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\) is a linear combination of \(\begin{pmatrix}3 \\ 1 \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 4\end{pmatrix}\text{,}\) and write \(\begin{pmatrix}b \\ -3 \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\) as a linear combination of \(\begin{pmatrix}3 \\ 1 \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 4\end{pmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We set up an augmented matrix and solve for \(b\) so that the corresponding system is consistent. We will swap the first and third rows to make the math easier, but also to make it easier to solve for \(b\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}3 \amp 1 \amp b\\ 1 \amp 0 \amp -3\\ 2 \amp 4 \amp 2\end{array}\right) \xrightarrow[\textup{then } R_1 = R_1 / 2]{R_1 \leftrightarrow R_3} \amp \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 2 \amp 1\\ 1 \amp 0 \amp -3\\ 3 \amp 1 \amp h\end{array}\right) \xrightarrow[R_3 = R_3 - 3R_1]{R_2 = R_2 - R_1} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 2 \amp 1\\ 0 \amp -2 \amp -4\\ 0 \amp -5 \amp h-3\end{array}\right)\\ \xrightarrow[]{R_2 = (-1/2)R_2} \amp \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 2 \amp 1\\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2\\ 0 \amp -5 \amp h-3\end{array}\right) \xrightarrow[]{R_3 = R_3 + 5R_2} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 2 \amp 1\\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2\\ 0 \amp 0 \amp h+7\end{array}\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
    The system is consistent when there is no pivot in the right column, so \(h+7=0\text{,}\) thus \(h = -7\text{.}\) To finish the problem, we need to solve
    \begin{gather*} x_1 \begin{pmatrix}3 \\ 1 \\ 2\end{pmatrix} + x_2 \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 4\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-7 \\ -3 \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    For this, we use the work that was already done above and do one more row operation.
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 2 \amp 1\\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2\\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0\end{array}\right) \xrightarrow[]{R_1 = R_1 - 2R_2} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 0 \amp -3\\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2\\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0\end{array}\right), \qquad x_1 = -3, \ x_2 = 2\text{.} \end{align*}
    \begin{gather*} -3 \begin{pmatrix}3 \\ 1 \\ 2\end{pmatrix} + 2 \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 4\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-7 \\ -3 \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗

    17.

    Let \(v_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\) and \(v_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 3\end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) Write a vector \(w\) in \({\bf R}^3\) that is not in \(\textup{Span}\{v_1, v_2\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since \(\textup{Span}\{v_1, v_2\}\) is the set of all linear combinations of \(v_1\) and \(v_2\text{,}\) we need to find a vector \(w\) that is not a linear combination of \(v_1\) and \(v_2\text{.}\) In other words, we need to find a \(w = \begin{pmatrix}a \\ b \\ c\end{pmatrix}\) so that the vector equation \(x_1v_1 + x_2 v_2 = w\) is inconsistent. We can put this in augmented matrix form and row-reduce.
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 0 \amp a \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp b \\ 2 \amp 3 \amp c\end{array}\right) \xrightarrow[R_3 = R_3 - 3R_2]{R_3 = R_3 - 2 R_1} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 0 \amp a \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp b \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp c-2a-3b\end{array}\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
    The system will be inconsistent precisely when the final column has a pivot, which is when \(c - 2a - 3b \neq 0\text{.}\) For example, the vector \(\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\) will make the system inconsistent since \(1-2(1)-3(1) = -4 \neq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Alternatively, we could have done this problem through trial and error. Since the system of equations is \(x_1 = a, \ x_2 = b, \ 2x_1 + 3x_2 = c\text{,}\) one vector that will make this inconsistent is \(\begin{pmatrix}a \\ b \\ c\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\text{,}\) since that requires \(x_1 = 0\) and \(x_2 = 0\) but \(2x_1 + 3x_2 = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗

    18.

    Find all values of \(c\) and \(d\) so that \(\textup{Span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ c\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}d \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\right\}\) is not \({\bf R}^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Neither of the vectors given is the zero vector, so their span is at least a line. The only way their span is just a line is if they are collinear. Therefore, the vectors will span \({\bf R}^2\) unless one is a scalar multiple of the other, which means there is some (nonzero) \(k\) so that \(d = 1k\) and \(2 = ck\text{,}\) which means
    \begin{gather*} k = d = 2/c\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Therefore, \(c\) is any nonzero real number and \(d = 2/c\text{.}\)

    🔗

    19.

    Consider the set \(V\) of all vectors in \({\bf R}^3\) of the form
    \begin{gather*} \begin{pmatrix}3x_1-x_2 \\ 2x_1+5x_2 \\ -10x_2\end{pmatrix} \quad (x_1, \ x_2 \textup{ real})\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Write \(V\) as a span of vectors in \({\bf R}^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Unwrapping the general form of a vector in \(V\text{,}\) we see
    \begin{gather*} \begin{pmatrix}3x_1-x_2 \\ 2x_1+5x_2 \\ -10x_2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}3x_1 \\ 2x_1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}-x_2 \\ 5x_2 \\ -10x_2\end{pmatrix} = x_1 \begin{pmatrix}3 \\ 2 \\ 0\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}-1 \\ 5 \\ -10\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Therefore, the vectors in \(V\) are simply all linear combinations of \(\begin{pmatrix}3 \\ 2 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix}-1 \\ 5 \\ -10\end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) In other words,
    \begin{gather*} V = \textup{Span}\left\{ \begin{pmatrix}3 \\ 2 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}-1 \\ 5 \\ -10\end{pmatrix}.\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
