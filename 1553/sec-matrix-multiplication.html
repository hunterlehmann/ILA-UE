    Section 3.4 Matrix Multiplication

    Objectives

    1. Understand compositions of transformations.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Understand the relationship between matrix products and compositions of matrix transformations.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Become comfortable doing basic algebra involving matrices.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Recipe: matrix multiplication (two ways).
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Picture: composition of transformations.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Vocabulary word: composition.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    In this section, we study compositions of transformations. As we will see, composition is a way of chaining transformations together. The composition of matrix transformations corresponds to a notion of multiplying two matrices together. We also discuss addition and scalar multiplication of transformations and of matrices.
    🔗

    Subsection 3.4.1 Composition of linear transformations

    Composition means the same thing in linear algebra as it does in Calculus. Here is the definition.
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.1.

    Let \(T\colon\R^n\to\R^m\) and \(U\colon\R^p\to\R^n\) be transformations. Their composition is the transformation \(T\circ U\colon\R^p\to\R^m\) defined by
    \begin{gather*} (T\circ U)(x) = T(U(x))\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Composing two transformations means chaining them together: \(T\circ U\) is the transformation that first applies \(U\text{,}\) then applies \(T\) (note the order of operations). More precisely, to evaluate \(T\circ U\) on an input vector \(x\text{,}\) first you evaluate \(U(x)\text{,}\) then you take this output vector of \(U\) and use it as an input vector of \(T\text{:}\) that is, \((T\circ U)(x) = T(U(x))\text{.}\) Of course, this only makes sense when the outputs of \(U\) are valid inputs of \(T\text{,}\) that is, when the range of \(U\) is contained in the domain of \(T\text{.}\)

    Insight 3.4.2 Domain and codomain of a composition.

    • In order for \(T\circ U\) to be defined, the codomain of \(U\) must equal the domain of \(T\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The domain of \(T\circ U\) is the domain of \(U\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The codomain of \(T\circ U\) is the codomain of \(T\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.3 Functions of one variable.

    Define \(f\colon\R\to\R\) by \(f(x) = x^2\) and \(g\colon\R\to\R\) by \(g(x) = x^3\text{.}\) The composition \(f\circ g\colon\R\to\R\) is the transformation defined by the rule
    \begin{gather*} f\circ g(x) = f(g(x)) = f(x^3) = (x^3)^2 = x^6\text{.} \end{gather*}
    For instance, \(f\circ g(-2) = f(-8) = 64.\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Interactive 3.4.4 A composition of matrix transformations.

    Define \(T\colon\R^3\to\R^2\) and \(U\colon\R^2\to\R^3\) by
    \begin{align*} T(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}x \sptxt{and} U(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}x\text{.} \end{align*}
    Their composition is a transformation \(T\circ U\colon\R^2\to\R^2\text{;}\) it turns out to be the matrix transformation associated to the matrix \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1\amp1\\1\amp1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Interactive 3.4.6 A transformation defined in steps.

    Let \(S\colon\R^3\to\R^3\) be the linear transformation that first reflects over the \(xy\)-plane and then projects onto the \(yz\)-plane, as in Example 20 of Section 3.3. The transformation \(S\) is the composition \(T\circ U\text{,}\) where \(U\colon\R^3\to\R^3\) is the transformation that reflects over the \(xy\)-plane, and \(T\colon\R^3\to\R^3\) is the transformation that projects onto the \(yz\)-plane.
    🔗
    🔗

    Interactive 3.4.8 A transformation defined in steps.

    Let \(S\colon\R^3\to\R^3\) be the linear transformation that first projects onto the \(xy\)-plane, and then projects onto the \(xz\)-plane. The transformation \(S\) is the composition \(T\circ U\text{,}\) where \(U\colon\R^3\to\R^3\) is the transformation that projects onto the \(xy\)-plane, and \(T\colon\R^3\to\R^3\) is the transformation that projects onto the \(xz\)-plane.
    🔗
    🔗
    Recall from Definition 20 of Section 3.1 that the identity transformation is the transformation \(\operatorname{Id}_{\R^n}\colon\R^n\to\R^n\) defined by \(\operatorname{Id}_{\R^n}(x) = x\) for every vector \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Note 3.4.10 Properties of composition.

    Let \(S,T,U\) be transformations and let \(c\) be a scalar. Suppose that \(T\colon\R^n\to\R^m\text{,}\) and that in each of the following identities, the domains and the codomains are compatible when necessary for the composition to be defined. The following properties are easily verified:
    \begin{align*} S\circ(T+U) \amp= S\circ T+S\circ U \amp (S + T)\circ U \amp= S\circ U + T\circ U \amp\\ c(T\circ U) \amp= (cT)\circ U \amp c(T\circ U) \amp= T\circ(cU) \rlap{\;\;\text{ if $T$ is linear}}\\ T\circ\operatorname{Id}_{\R^n} \amp= T \amp \operatorname{Id}_{\R^m}\circ T \amp= T\\ \amp \amp S\circ(T\circ U) \amp= (S\circ T)\circ U \amp \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The final property is called associativity. Unwrapping both sides, it says:
    \begin{gather*} S\circ(T\circ U)(x) = S(T\circ U(x)) = S(T(U(x))) = S\circ T(U(x)) = (S\circ T)\circ U(x)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    In other words, both \(S\circ (T\circ U)\) and \((S\circ T)\circ U\) are the transformation defined by first applying \(U\text{,}\) then \(T\text{,}\) then \(S\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Insight 3.4.11.

    Composition of transformations is not commutative in general. That is, in general, \(T\circ U\neq U\circ T\text{,}\) even when both compositions are defined.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.12 Functions of one variable.

    Define \(f\colon\R\to\R\) by \(f(x) = x^2\) and \(g\colon\R\to\R\) by \(g(x) = e^x\text{.}\) The composition \(f\circ g\colon\R\to\R\) is the transformation defined by the rule
    \begin{gather*} f\circ g(x) = f(g(x)) = f(e^x) = (e^x)^2 = e^{2x}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    The composition \(g\circ f\colon\R\to\R\) is the transformation defined by the rule
    \begin{gather*} g\circ f(x) = g(f(x)) = g(x^2) = e^{x^2}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Note that \(e^{x^2}\neq e^{2x}\) in general; for instance, if \(x=1\) then \(e^{x^2} = e\) and \(e^{2x} = e^2\text{.}\) Thus \(f \circ g\) is not equal to \(g \circ f\text{,}\) and we can already see with functions of one variable that composition of functions is not commutative.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.13 Non-commutative composition of transformations.

    Define matrix transformations \(T,U\colon\R^2\to\R^2\) by
    \begin{align*} T(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}x \sptxt{and} U(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}x\text{.} \end{align*}
    Geometrically, \(T\) is a shear in the \(x\)-direction, and \(U\) is a shear in the \(Y\)-direction. We evaluate
    \begin{gather*} T\circ U\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = T\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \end{gather*}
    and
    \begin{gather*} U\circ T\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = U\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Since \(T\circ U\) and \(U\circ T\) have different outputs for the input vector \(1\choose 0\text{,}\) they are different transformations. (See Example 23.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.4.2 Matrix multiplication

    In this subsection, we introduce a seemingly unrelated operation on matrices, namely, matrix multiplication. As we will see in the next subsection, matrix multiplication exactly corresponds to the composition of the corresponding linear transformations. First we need some terminology.
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.16.

    Let \(A\) be an \(m\times n\) matrix. We will generally write \(a_{ij}\) for the entry in the \(i\)th row and the \(j\)th column. It is called the \(i,j\) entry of the matrix. \def\spvdots{\vphantom{\vbox{\hbox{(}\kern0pt}}\smash{\vdots}} \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.2] \matrix[math matrix] (aij) { a_{11} \& \cdots \& a_{1j} \& \cdots \& a_{1n} \\ \spvdots \& \& \spvdots \& \& \spvdots \\ a_{i1} \& \cdots \& a_{ij} \& \cdots \& a_{in} \\ \spvdots \& \& \spvdots \& \& \spvdots \\ a_{m1} \& \cdots \& a_{mj} \& \cdots \& a_{mn} \& \\ }; \node[fit=(aij-1-3) (aij-5-3), inner sep=2pt, draw=blue!50, thick, rounded corners, label={[text=blue!50]below:\small$j$th column}] {}; \node[fit=(aij-3-1) (aij-3-5), inner sep=2pt, draw=green!70!black, thick, rounded corners] (row) {}; \node[text=green!70!black, rotate=90, anchor=north, yshift=.5mm, font=\small] at (row.east) {$i$th row}; \end{tikzpicture}
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.17 Matrix multiplication.

    Let \(A\) be an \(m\times n\) matrix and let \(B\) be an \(n\times p\) matrix. Denote the columns of \(B\) by \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_p\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} B = \begin{pmatrix} | \amp | \amp | \\ v_1 \amp v_2 \amp \cdots \amp v_p \\ | \amp | \amp | \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The product \(AB\) is the \(m\times p\) matrix with columns \(Av_1,Av_2,\ldots,Av_p\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} AB = \begin{pmatrix} | \amp | \amp | \\ Av_1 \amp Av_2 \amp \cdots \amp Av_p \\ | \amp | \amp | \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    In other words, matrix multiplication is defined column-by-column, or “distributes over the columns of \(B\text{.}\)”

    The row-column rule for matrix multiplication.

    Recall from Definition 9 of Section 2.3 that the product of a row vector and a column vector is the scalar
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \amp a_2 \amp \cdots \amp a_n \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{pmatrix} = a_1x_1 + a_2x_2 + \cdots + a_nx_n\text{.} \end{align*}

    Insight 3.4.22 Matrix multiplication caveats.

    • Matrix multiplication is not commutative: \(AB\) is not usually equal to \(BA\text{,}\) even when both products are defined and have the same size. See Example 23.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Matrix multiplication does not satisfy the cancellation law: \(AB=AC\) does not imply \(B=C\text{,}\) even when \(A\neq 0\text{.}\) For example,
      \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 5 \amp 6 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • It is possible for \(AB=0\text{,}\) even when \(A\neq 0\) and \(B\neq 0\text{.}\) For example,
      \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    While matrix multiplication is not commutative in general there are examples of matrices \(A\) and \(B\) with \(AB=BA\text{.}\) For example, this always works when \(A\) is the zero matrix, or when \(A=B\text{.}\) The reader is encouraged to find other examples.

    Example 3.4.23 Non-commutative multiplication of matrices.

    Consider the matrices
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix} \sptxt{and} B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{,} \end{align*}
    as in Example 13. The matrix \(AB\) is
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{,} \end{align*}
    whereas the matrix \(BA\) is
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    In particular, we have
    \begin{gather*} AB \neq BA\text{.} \end{gather*}
    And so matrix multiplication is not always commutative. It is not a coincidence that this example agrees with Example 13; we are about to see that multiplication of matrices corresponds to composition of transformations.

    Example 3.4.24 Order of Operations.

    Let \(T\colon\R^n\to\R^m\) and \(U\colon\R^p\to\R^n\) be linear transformations, and let \(A\) and \(B\) be their standard matrices, respectively. Recall that \(T\circ U(x)\) is the vector obtained by first applying \(U\) to \(x\text{,}\) and then \(T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On the matrix side, the standard matrix of \(T\circ U\) is the product \(AB\text{,}\) so \(T\circ U(x) = (AB)x\text{.}\) By associativity of matrix multiplication, we have \((AB)x = A(Bx)\text{,}\) so the product \((AB)x\) can be computed by first multiplying \(x\) by \(B\text{,}\) then multipyling the product by \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Therefore, matrix multiplication happens in the same order as composition of transformations. In other words, both matrices and transformations are written in the order opposite from the order in which they act. But matrix multiplication and composition of transformations are written in the same order as each other: the matrix for \(T\circ U\) is \(AB\text{.}\)

    Subsection 3.4.3 Composition and Matrix Multiplication

    The point of this subsection is to show that matrix multiplication corresponds to composition of transformations, that is, the standard matrix for \(T \circ U\) is the product of the standard matrices for \(T\) and for \(U\text{.}\) It should be hard to believe that our complicated formula for matrix multiplication actually means something intuitive such as “chaining two transformations together”!
    🔗

    Theorem 3.4.25.

    Let \(T\colon\R^n\to\R^m\) and \(U\colon\R^p\to\R^n\) be linear transformations, and let \(A\) and \(B\) be their standard matrices, respectively, so \(A\) is an \(m\times n\) matrix and \(B\) is an \(n\times p\) matrix. Then \(T\circ U\colon\R^p\to\R^m\) is a linear transformation, and its standard matrix is the product \(AB\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    First we verify that \(T\circ U\) is linear. Let \(u,v\) be vectors in \(\R^p\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} T\circ U(u+v) \amp= T(U(u+v)) = T(U(u)+U(v))\\ \amp= T(U(u))+T(U(v)) = T\circ U(u) + T\circ U(v)\text{.} \end{align*}
    If \(c\) is a scalar, then
    \begin{gather*} T\circ U(cv) = T(U(cv)) = T(cU(v)) = cT(U(v)) = cT\circ U(v)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Since \(T\circ U\) satisfies the two Definition 1 of Section 3.3, it is a linear transformation.
    🔗
    Now that we know that \(T\circ U\) is linear, it makes sense to compute its standard matrix. Let \(C\) be the standard matrix of \(T\circ U\text{,}\) so \(T(x) = Ax,\) \(U(x) = Bx\text{,}\) and \(T\circ U(x) = Cx\text{.}\) By Theorem 14 of Section 3.3, the first column of \(C\) is \(Ce_1\text{,}\) and the first column of \(B\) is \(Be_1\text{.}\) We have
    \begin{gather*} T\circ U(e_1) = T(U(e_1)) = T(Be_1) = A(Be_1)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    By definition, the first column of the product \(AB\) is the product of \(A\) with the first column of \(B\text{,}\) which is \(Be_1\text{,}\) so
    \begin{gather*} Ce_1 = T\circ U(e_1) = A(Be_1) = (AB)e_1\text{.} \end{gather*}
    It follows that \(C\) has the same first column as \(AB\text{.}\) The same argument as applied to the \(i\)th standard coordinate vector \(e_i\) shows that \(C\) and \(AB\) have the same \(i\)th column; since they have the same columns, they are the same matrix.
    🔗
    🔗
    The theorem justifies our choice of definition of the matrix product. This is the one and only reason that matrix products are defined in this way. To rephrase:
    🔗

    Insight 3.4.26 Products and compositions.

    The matrix of the composition of two linear transformations is the product of the matrices of the transformations.

    Example 3.4.27 Composition of rotations.

    In Example 18 of Section 3.3, we showed that the standard matrix for the counterclockwise rotation of the plane by an angle of \(\theta\) is

    Let \(T\colon\R^2\to\R^2\) be counterclockwise rotation by \(45^\circ\text{,}\) and let \(U\colon\R^2\to\R^2\) be counterclockwise rotation by \(90^\circ\text{.}\) The matrices \(A\) and \(B\) for \(T\) and \(U\) are, respectively,

    Subsection 3.4.4 The algebra of transformations and matrices

    In this subsection we describe two more operations that one can perform on transformations: addition and scalar multiplication. We then translate these operations into the language of matrices. This is analogous to what we did for the composition of linear transformations, but much less subtle.
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.33.

    • Let \(T,U\colon\R^n\to\R^m\) be two transformations. Their sum is the transformation \(T+U\colon\R^n\to\R^m\) defined by
      \begin{gather*} (T+U)(x) = T(x) + U(x)\text{.} \end{gather*}
      Note that addition of transformations is only defined when both transformations have the same domain and codomain.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Let \(T\colon\R^n\to\R^m\) be a transformation, and let \(c\) be a scalar. The scalar product of \(c\) with \(T\) is the transformation \(cT\colon\R^n\to\R^m\) defined by
      \begin{gather*} (cT)(x) = c\cdot T(x)\text{.} \end{gather*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    To emphasize, the sum of two transformations \(T,U\colon\R^n\to\R^m\) is another transformation called \(T+U\text{;}\) its value on an input vector \(x\) is the sum of the outputs of \(T\) and \(U\text{.}\) Similarly, the product of \(T\) with a scalar \(c\) is another transformation called \(cT\text{;}\) its value on an input vector \(x\) is the vector \(c\cdot T(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 3.4.34 Functions of one variable.

    Define \(f\colon\R\to\R\) by \(f(x) = x^2\) and \(g\colon\R\to\R\) by \(g(x) = x^3\text{.}\) The sum \(f+g\colon\R\to\R\) is the transformation defined by the rule
    \begin{gather*} (f+g)(x) = f(x) + g(x) = x^2 + x^3\text{.} \end{gather*}
    For instance, \((f+g)(-2) = (-2)^2 + (-2)^3 = -4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Define \(\exp\colon\R\to\R\) by \(\exp(x) = e^x\text{.}\) The product \(2\exp\colon\R\to\R\) is the transformation defined by the rule
    \begin{gather*} (2\exp)(x) = 2\cdot\exp(x) = 2e^x\text{.} \end{gather*}
    For instance, \((2\exp)(1) = 2\cdot\exp(1) = 2e\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 3.4.35 Properties of addition and scalar multiplication for transformations.

    Let \(S,T,U\colon\R^n\to\R^m\) be transformations and let \(c,d\) be scalars. The following properties are easily verified:
    \begin{align*} T + U \amp= U + T \amp S + (T + U) \amp= (S + T) + U\\ c(T + U) \amp= cT + cU \amp (c + d)T \amp= cT + dT\\ c(dT) \amp= (cd)T \amp T + 0 \amp= T \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    In one of the above properties, we used \(0\) to denote the transformation \(\R^n\to\R^m\) that is zero on every input vector: \(0(x) = 0\) for all \(x\text{.}\) This is called the zero transformation.
    🔗
    We now give the analogous operations for matrices.
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.36.

    • The sum of two \(m\times n\) matrices is the matrix obtained by summing the entries of \(A\) and \(B\) individually:
      \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} \amp a_{12} \amp a_{13} \\ a_{21} \amp a_{22} \amp a_{23} \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} b_{11} \amp b_{12} \amp b_{13} \\ b_{21} \amp b_{22} \amp b_{23} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11}+b_{11} \amp a_{12}+b_{12} \amp a_{13}+b_{13} \\ a_{21}+b_{21} \amp a_{22}+b_{22} \amp a_{23}+b_{23} \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
      In other words, the \(i,j\) entry of \(A+B\) is the sum of the \(i,j\) entries of \(A\) and \(B\text{.}\) Note that addition of matrices is only defined when both matrices have the same size.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The scalar product of a scalar \(c\) with a matrix \(A\) is obtained by scaling all entries of \(A\) by \(c\text{:}\)
      \begin{align*} \def\mh{\textcolor{red}} \mh{c\begin{pmatrix}} a_{11} \amp a_{12} \amp a_{13} \\ a_{21} \amp a_{22} \amp a_{23} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \amp ca_{11} \amp \amp ca_{12} \amp \amp ca_{13} \\ \amp ca_{21} \amp \amp ca_{22} \amp \amp ca_{23} \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
      In other words, the \(i,j\) entry of \(cA\) is \(c\) times the \(i,j\) entry of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Fact 3.4.37.

    Let \(T,U\colon\R^n\to\R^m\) be linear transformations with standard matrices \(A,B\text{,}\) respectively, and let \(c\) be a scalar.
    • The standard matrix for \(T+U\) is \(A+B\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The standard matrix for \(cT\) is \(cA\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In view of the above fact, the following properties are consequences of the corresponding Note 35 of transformations. They are easily verified directly from the definitions as well.
    🔗

    Note 3.4.38 Properties of addition and scalar multiplication for matrices.

    Let \(A,B,C\) be \(m\times n\) matrices and let \(c,d\) be scalars. Then:
    \begin{align*} A + B \amp= B + A \amp C + (A + B) \amp= (C + A) + B\\ c(A + B) \amp= cA + cB \amp (c + d)A \amp= cA + dA\\ c(dA) \amp= (cd)A \amp A + 0 \amp= A \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    In one of the above properties, we used \(0\) to denote the \(m\times n\) matrix whose entries are all zero. This is the standard matrix of the zero transformation, and is called the zero matrix.
    🔗
    We can also combine addition and scalar multiplication of matrices with multiplication of matrices. Since matrix multiplication corresponds to composition of transformations (Theorem 25), the following properties are consequences of the corresponding Note 10 of transformations.
    🔗

    Note 3.4.39 Properties of matrix multiplication.

    Let \(A,B,C\) be matrices and let \(c\) be a scalar. Suppose that \(A\) is an \(m\times n\) matrix, and that in each of the following identities, the sizes of \(B\) and \(C\) are compatible when necessary for the product to be defined. Then:
    \begin{align*} C(A+B) \amp= C A+C B \amp (A + B) C \amp= A C + B C \amp\\ c(A B) \amp= (cA) B \amp c(A B) \amp= A(cB)\\ A I_n \amp= A \amp I_m A \amp= A\\ (A B)C \amp= A (BC) \amp \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Most of the above properties are easily verified directly from the definitions. The associativity property \((AB)C=A(BC)\text{,}\) however, is not (try it!). It is much easier to prove by relating matrix multiplication to composition of transformations, and using the obvious fact that composition of transformations is associative.

    Consider the following transformations.
    🔗
    \(T: {\bf R}^2 \to {\bf R}^2\) be rotation by \(90^\circ\) clockwise.
    🔗
    \(U: {\bf R}^3 \to {\bf R}^2\) given by \(U(a, b, c) = (a-b, c-5a)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(V: {\bf R}^{4} \to {\bf R}^3\) given by \(V(x, y, z, w) = (w, x-y+z, z-x)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Which composition of all three transformation makes sense?
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(T \circ U \circ V\) makes sense: \(V\) sends a vector \(x\) from \({\bf R}^4\) to \({\bf R}^3\text{,}\) then \(U\) sends \(V(x)\) from \({\bf R}^3\) to \({\bf R}^2\text{,}\) then \(T\) sends \(U(V(x))\) from \({\bf R}^2\) to \({\bf R}^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Using matrix multiplication, compute the standard matrix for the transformation you wrote in part (a).
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(T(x) = Ax\text{,}\) \(U(x) = Bx\text{,}\) and \(V(x) = Cx\) for the matrices below, where we multiply \(ABC\) to get the matrix for \(T \circ U \circ V\text{.}\) Matrix multiplication is NOT commutative, but it is associative, so to compute \(ABC\) we can either compute \(A(BC)\) or \((AB)C\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} ABC \amp = \begin{pmatrix}0 \amp 1\\ -1 \amp 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1 \amp -1 \amp 0\\ -5 \amp 0 \amp 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 1\\ 1 \amp -1 \amp 1 \amp 0\\ -1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 0\end{pmatrix}\\ \amp = \begin{pmatrix}0 \amp 1\\ -1 \amp 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}-1 \amp 1 \amp -1 \amp 1\\ -1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp -5\end{pmatrix}\\ \amp = \begin{pmatrix}-1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 5\\ 1 \amp -1 \amp 1 \amp -1\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
