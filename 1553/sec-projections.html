    Section 6.3 Orthogonal Projection

    Objectives

    1. Understand the orthogonal decomposition of a vector with respect to a subspace.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Understand the relationship between orthogonal decomposition and orthogonal projection.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Understand the relationship between orthogonal decomposition and the closest vector on / distance to a subspace.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Learn the basic properties of orthogonal projections as linear transformations and as matrix transformations.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Recipes: orthogonal projection onto a line, orthogonal decomposition by solving a system of equations, orthogonal projection via a complicated matrix product.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Pictures: orthogonal decomposition, orthogonal projection.
      🔗
      🔗
    7. Vocabulary words: orthogonal decomposition, orthogonal projection.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Let \(W\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\) and let \(x\) be a vector in \(\R^n\text{.}\) In this section, we will learn to compute the closest vector \(x_W\) to \(x\) in \(W\text{.}\) The vector \(x_W\) is called the orthogonal projection of \(x\) onto \(W\text{.}\) This is exactly what we will use to almost solve matrix equations, as discussed in the introduction to Chapter 6.
    🔗

    Subsection 6.3.1 Orthogonal Decomposition

    We begin by fixing some notation.
    🔗

    Definition 6.3.1.

    Let \(W\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\) and let \(x\) be a vector in \(\R^n\text{.}\) We denote the closest vector to \(x\) on \(W\) by \(x_W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    To say that \(x_W\) is the closest vector to \(x\) on \(W\) means that the difference \(x-x_W\) is orthogonal to the vectors in \(W\text{:}\)
    🔗
    
    🔗
    In other words, if \(x_{W^\perp} = x - x_W\text{,}\) then we have \(x = x_W + x_{W^\perp}\text{,}\) where \(x_W\) is in \(W\) and \(x_{W^\perp}\) is in \(W^\perp\text{.}\) The first order of business is to prove that the closest vector always exists.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.3.2 Orthogonal decomposition.

    Let \(W\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\) and let \(x\) be a vector in \(\R^n\text{.}\) Then we can write \(x\) uniquely as
    \begin{gather*} x = x_W + x_{W^\perp} \end{gather*}
    where \(x_W\) is the closest vector to \(x\) on \(W\) and \(x_{W^\perp}\) is in \(W^\perp\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(m = \dim(W)\text{,}\) so \(n-m = \dim(W^\perp)\) by Fact 16 of Section 6.2. Let \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\) be a basis for \(W\) and let \(v_{m+1},v_{m+2},\ldots,v_n\) be a basis for \(W^\perp\text{.}\) We showed in the proof of Fact 16 of Section 6.2 that \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m,v_{m+1},v_{m+2},\ldots,v_n\}\) is linearly independent, so it forms a basis for \(\R^n\text{.}\) Therefore, we can write
    \begin{gather*} x = (c_1v_1 + \cdots + c_mv_m) + (c_{m+1}v_{m+1} + \cdots + c_nv_n) = x_W + x_{W^\perp}\text{,} \end{gather*}
    where \(x_W = c_1v_1 + \cdots + c_mv_m\) and \(x_{W^\perp} = c_{m+1}v_{m+1} + \cdots + c_nv_n\text{.}\) Since \(x_{W^\perp}\) is orthogonal to \(W\text{,}\) the vector \(x_W\) is the closest vector to \(x\) on \(W\text{,}\) so this proves that such a decomposition exists.
    🔗
    As for uniqueness, suppose that
    \begin{gather*} x = x_W + x_{W^\perp} = y_W + y_{W^\perp} \end{gather*}
    for \(x_W,y_W\) in \(W\) and \(x_{W^\perp},y_{W^\perp}\) in \(W^\perp\text{.}\) Rearranging gives
    \begin{gather*} x_W - y_W = y_{W^\perp} - x_{W^\perp}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Since \(W\) and \(W^\perp\) are subspaces, the left side of the equation is in \(W\) and the right side is in \(W^\perp\text{.}\) Therefore, \(x_W-y_W\) is in \(W\) and in \(W^\perp\text{,}\) so it is orthogonal to itself, which implies \(x_W-y_W=0\text{.}\) Hence \(x_W = y_W\) and \(x_{W^\perp} = y_{W^\perp}\text{,}\) which proves uniqueness.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 6.3.3.

    Let \(W\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\) and let \(x\) be a vector in \(\R^n\text{.}\) The expression
    \begin{gather*} x = x_W + x_{W^\perp} \end{gather*}
    for \(x_W\) in \(W\) and \(x_{W^\perp}\) in \(W^\perp\text{,}\) is called the orthogonal decomposition of \(x\) with respect to \(W\text{,}\) and the closest vector \(x_W\) is the orthogonal projection of \(x\) onto \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Since \(x_W\) is the closest vector on \(W\) to \(x\text{,}\) the distance from \(x\) to the subspace \(W\) is the length of the vector from \(x_W\) to \(x\text{,}\) i.e., the length of \(x_{W^\perp}\text{.}\) To restate:
    🔗

    Closest vector and distance.

    Let \(W\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\) and let \(x\) be a vector in \(\R^n\text{.}\)
    • The orthogonal projection \(x_W\) is the closest vector to \(x\) in \(W\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The distance from \(x\) to \(W\) is \(\|x_{W^\perp}\|\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.3.4 Orthogonal decomposition with respect to the \(xy\)-plane.

    Let \(W\) be the \(xy\)-plane in \(\R^3\text{,}\) so \(W^\perp\) is the \(z\)-axis. It is easy to compute the orthogonal decomposition of a vector with respect to this \(W\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} x \amp= \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \implies x_W = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \quad x_{W^\perp} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\\ x \amp= \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{pmatrix} \implies x_W = \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \quad x_{W^\perp} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ c \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    We see that the orthogonal decomposition in this case expresses a vector in terms of a “horizontal” component (in the \(xy\)-plane) and a “vertical” component (on the \(z\)-axis).
    🔗
    
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.3.6 Orthogonal decomposition of a vector in \(W\).

    If \(x\) is in a subspace \(W\text{,}\) then the closest vector to \(x\) in \(W\) is itself, so \(x = x_W\) and \(x_{W^\perp} = 0\text{.}\) Conversely, if \(x = x_W\) then \(x\) is contained in \(W\) because \(x_W\) is contained in \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.3.7 Orthogonal decomposition of a vector in \(W^\perp\).

    If \(W\) is a subspace and \(x\) is in \(W^\perp\text{,}\) then the orthogonal decomposition of \(x\) is \(x = 0 + x\text{,}\) where \(0\) is in \(W\) and \(x\) is in \(W^\perp\text{.}\) It follows that \(x_W = 0\text{.}\) Conversely, if \(x_W = 0\) then the orthogonal decomposition of \(x\) is \(x = x_W + x_{W^\perp} = 0 + x_{W^\perp}\text{,}\) so \(x = x_{W^\perp}\) is in \(W^\perp\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    🔗
    Now we turn to the problem of computing \(x_W\) and \(x_{W^\perp}\text{.}\) Of course, since \(x_{W^\perp} = x - x_W\text{,}\) really all we need is to compute \(x_W\text{.}\) The following theorem gives a method for computing the orthogonal projection onto a column space. To compute the orthogonal projection onto a general subspace, usually it is best to rewrite the subspace as the column space of a matrix, as in Note .
    🔗

    Theorem 6.3.14.

    Let \(A\) be an \(m \times n\) matrix, let \(W = \Col(A)\text{,}\) and let \(x\) be a vector in \(\R^m\text{.}\) Then the matrix equation
    \begin{gather*} A^TAc=A^Tx \end{gather*}
    in the unknown vector \(c\) is consistent, and \(x_W\) is equal to \(Ac\) for any solution \(c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(x = x_W + x_{W^\perp}\) be the orthogonal decomposition with respect to \(W\text{.}\) By definition \(x_W\) lies in \(W=\Col(A)\) and so there is a vector \(c\) in \(\R^n\) with \(Ac = x_W\text{.}\) Choose any such vector \(c\text{.}\) We know that \(x-x_W=x-Ac\) lies in \(W^\perp\text{,}\) which is equal to \(\Nul(A^T)\) by Note . We thus have
    \begin{gather*} 0=A^T(x-Ac) = A^Tx-A^TAc \end{gather*}
    and so
    \begin{gather*} A^TAc = A^Tx\text{.} \end{gather*}
    This exactly means that \(A^TAc = A^Tx\) is consistent. If \(c\) is any solution to \(A^TAc=A^Tx\) then by reversing the above logic, we conclude that \(x_W = Ac\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.3.15 Orthogonal projection onto a line.

    Let \(L = \Span\{u\}\) be a line in \(\R^n\) and let \(x\) be a vector in \(\R^n\text{.}\) By the Theorem 14, to find \(x_L\) we must solve the matrix equation \(u^Tuc = u^Tx\text{,}\) where we regard \(u\) as an \(n\times 1\) matrix (the column space of this matrix is exactly \(L\text{!}\)). But \(u^Tu = u\cdot u\) and \(u^Tx = u\cdot x\text{,}\) so \(c = (u\cdot x)/(u\cdot u)\) is a solution of \(u^Tuc = u^Tx\text{,}\) and hence \(x_L = uc = (u\cdot x)/(u\cdot u)\,u.\)
    🔗
    
    🔗
    🔗
    To reiterate:
    🔗

    Recipe 6.3.16 Orthogonal projection onto a line.

    If \(L = \Span\{u\}\) is a line, then
    \begin{gather*} x_L = \frac{u\cdot x}{u\cdot u}\,u \sptxt{and} x_{L^\perp} = x - x_L \end{gather*}
    for any vector \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 6.3.17 Simple proof for the formula for projection onto a line.

    In the special case where we are projecting a vector \(x\) in \(\R^n\) onto a line \(L = \Span\{u\}\text{,}\) our formula for the projection can be derived very directly and simply. The vector \(x_L\) is a multiple of \(u\text{,}\) say \(x_L=cu\text{.}\) This multiple is chosen so that \(x-x_L=x-cu\) is perpendicular to \(u\text{,}\) as in the following picture.
    🔗
    
    🔗
    In other words,
    \begin{gather*} (x-cu) \cdot u = 0\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Using the distributive property for the dot product and isolating the variable \(c\) gives us that
    \begin{gather*} c = \frac{u\cdot x}{u\cdot u} \end{gather*}
    and so
    \begin{gather*} x_L = cu = \frac{u\cdot x}{u\cdot u}\,u\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.3.18 Projection onto a line in \(\R^2\).

    Compute the orthogonal projection of \(x = {-6\choose 4}\) onto the line \(L\) spanned by \(u = {3\choose 2}\text{,}\) and find the distance from \(x\) to \(L\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    First we find
    \begin{gather*} x_L = \frac{x\cdot u}{u\cdot u}\,u = \frac{-18+8}{9+4}\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = -\frac{10}{13}\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \qquad x_{L^\perp} = x - x_L = \frac 1{13}\begin{pmatrix} -48 \\ 72 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    The distance from \(x\) to \(L\) is
    \begin{gather*} \|x_{L^\perp}\| = \frac 1{13}\sqrt{48^2 + 72^2} \approx 6.656\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.3.20 Projection onto a line in \(\R^3\).

    Let
    \begin{gather*} x = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} \qquad u = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{,} \end{gather*}
    and let \(L\) be the line spanned by \(u\text{.}\) Compute \(x_L\) and \(x_L^\perp\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    \begin{gather*} x_L = \frac{x\cdot u}{u\cdot u}\,u = \frac{2+3-1}{1+1+1}\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{4}{3}\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \qquad x_{L^\perp} = x - x_L = \frac 13\begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 5 \\ -7 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    When \(A\) is a matrix with more than one column, computing the orthogonal projection of \(x\) onto \(W = \Col(A)\) means solving the matrix equation \(A^TAc = A^Tx\text{.}\) In other words, we can compute the closest vector by solving a system of linear equations. To be explicit, we state the Theorem 14 as a recipe:
    🔗

    Recipe 6.3.22 Compute an orthogonal decomposition.

    Let \(W\) be a subspace of \(\R^m\text{.}\) Here is a method to compute the orthogonal decomposition of a vector \(x\) with respect to \(W\text{:}\)
    1. Rewrite \(W\) as the column space of a matrix \(A\text{.}\) In other words, find a a spanning set for \(W\text{,}\) and let \(A\) be the matrix with those columns.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Compute the matrix \(A^TA\) and the vector \(A^Tx\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Form the augmented matrix for the matrix equation \(A^TAc = A^Tx\) in the unknown vector \(c\text{,}\) and row reduce.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. This equation is always consistent; choose one solution \(c\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{gather*} x_W = Ac \qquad x_{W^\perp} = x - x_W\text{.} \end{gather*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.3.23 Projection onto the \(xy\)-plane.

    Use the Theorem 14 to compute the orthogonal decomposition of a vector with respect to the \(xy\)-plane in \(\R^3\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    A basis for the \(xy\)-plane is given by the two standard coordinate vectors
    \begin{gather*} e_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \qquad e_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Let \(A\) be the matrix with columns \(e_1,e_2\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Then
    \begin{align*} A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix} = I_2 \qquad A^T\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    It follows that the unique solution \(c\) of \(A^TAc = I_2c = A^Tx\) is given by the first two coordinates of \(x\text{,}\) so
    \begin{align*} x_W = A\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \qquad x_{W^\perp} = x - x_W = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    We have recovered Example 4.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.3.24 Projection onto a plane in \(\R^3\).

    Let
    \begin{gather*} W = \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\right\} \qquad x = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Compute \(x_W\) and the distance from \(x\) to \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    We have to solve the matrix equation \(A^TAc = A^Tx\text{,}\) where
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    We have
    \begin{align*} A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix} \qquad A^Tx = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    We form an augmented matrix and row reduce:
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 2 \amp 1 \amp -2 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 3 \end{array}\right) \rref \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 \amp 0 \amp -7/3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 8/3 \end{array}\right) \implies c = \frac 13\begin{pmatrix} -7 \\ 8 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    It follows that
    \begin{gather*} x_W = Ac = \frac 13\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 8 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix} \qquad x_{W^\perp} = x - x_W = \frac 13\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    The distance from \(x\) to \(W\) is
    \begin{gather*} \|x_{W^\perp}\| = \frac 1{3}\sqrt{4+4+4} \approx 1.155\text{.} \end{gather*}

    Example 6.3.26 Projection onto another plane in \(\R^3\).

    Let
    \begin{gather*} W = \left\{\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix}\bigm|x_1 - 2x_2 = x_3\right\} \sptxt{and} x = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Compute \(x_W\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    Method 1: First we need to find a spanning set for \(W\text{.}\) We notice that \(W\) is the solution set of the homogeneous equation \(x_1 - 2x_2 - x_3 = 0\text{,}\) so \(W = \Nul\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -2 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) We know how to compute a basis for a null space: we row reduce and find the parametric vector form. The matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -2 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\) is already in reduced row echelon form. The parametric form is \(x_1 = 2x_2 + x_3\text{,}\) so the parametric vector form is
    \begin{gather*} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix} = x_2\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + x_3\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{,} \end{gather*}
    and hence a basis for \(V\) is given by
    \begin{gather*} \left\{\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    We let \(A\) be the matrix whose columns are our basis vectors:
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Hence \(\Col(A) = \Nul\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -2 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix} = W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now we can continue with step 1 of the recipe. We compute
    \begin{align*} A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix} \qquad A^Tx = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    We write the linear system \(A^TAc = A^Tx\) as an augmented matrix and row reduce:
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 5 \amp 2 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 2 \amp 2 \end{array}\right) \rref \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 \amp 0 \amp 1/3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2/3 \end{array}\right)\text{.} \end{align*}
    Hence we can take \(c = {1/3\choose 2/3}\text{,}\) so
    \begin{align*} x_W = Ac = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1/3 \\ 2/3 \end{pmatrix} = \frac 13\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Method 2: In this case, it is easier to compute \(x_{W^\perp}\text{.}\) Indeed, since \(W = \Nul\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -2 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix},\) the orthogonal complement is the line
    \begin{gather*} V = W^\perp = \Col\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Using the formula for Example 15 gives
    \begin{gather*} x_{W^\perp} = x_V = \frac{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}}{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac 13\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Hence we have
    \begin{gather*} x_W = x - x_{W^\perp} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} - \frac 13\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. = \frac 13\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\text{,} \end{gather*}
    as above.

    Example 6.3.28 Projection onto a \(3\)-space in \(\R^4\).

    Let
    \begin{gather*} W = \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\right\} \qquad x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Compute the orthogonal decomposition of \(x\) with respect to \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗

    We compute
    \begin{align*} A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 2 \amp 4 \end{pmatrix} \qquad A^Tx = \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ -3 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    We form an augmented matrix and row reduce:
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 2 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp -3 \\ 0 \amp 2 \amp 2 \amp -3 \\ 0 \amp 2 \amp 4 \amp 0 \end{array}\right) \rref \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp -3/2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp -3 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 3/2 \end{array}\right) \implies c = \frac 12\begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ -6 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    It follows that
    \begin{gather*} x_W = Ac = \frac 12\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -3 \\ 6 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \qquad x_{W^\perp} = \frac 12\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 5 \\ 0 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}

    🔗

    Corollary 6.3.29.

    Let \(A\) be an \(m \times n\) matrix with linearly independent columns and let \(W = \Col(A)\text{.}\) Then the \(n\times n\) matrix \(A^TA\) is invertible, and for all vectors \(x\) in \(\R^m\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{gather*} x_W = A(A^TA)\inv A^Tx\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    We will show that \(\Nul(A^TA)=\{0\}\text{,}\) which implies invertibility by the Theorem 31 of Section 5.1. Suppose that \(A^TAc = 0\text{.}\) Then \(A^TAc = A^T0\text{,}\) so \(0_W = Ac\) by the Theorem 14. But \(0_W = 0\) (the orthogonal decomposition of the zero vector is just \(0 = 0 + 0)\text{,}\) so \(Ac = 0\text{,}\) and therefore \(c\) is in \(\Nul(A)\text{.}\) Since the columns of \(A\) are linearly independent, we have \(c=0\text{,}\) so \(\Nul(A^TA)=0\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗
    Let \(x\) be a vector in \(\R^n\) and let \(c\) be a solution of \(A^TAc = A^Tx\text{.}\) Then \(c = (A^TA)\inv A^Tx\text{,}\) so \(x_W =Ac = A(A^TA)\inv A^Tx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The corollary applies in particular to the case where we have a subspace \(W\) of \(\R^m\text{,}\) and a basis \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\) for \(W\text{.}\) To apply the corollary, we take \(A\) to be the \(m\times n\) matrix with columns \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\text{.}\)

    Subsection 6.3.2 Orthogonal Projection

    In this subsection, we change perspective and think of the orthogonal projection \(x_W\) as a function of \(x\text{.}\) This function turns out to be a linear transformation with many nice properties, and is a good example of a linear transformation which is not originally defined as a matrix transformation.
    🔗

    Proposition 6.3.31 Properties of Orthogonal Projections.

    Let \(W\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\text{,}\) and define \(T\colon\R^n\to\R^n\) by \(T(x) = x_W\text{.}\) Then:
    1. \(T\) is a linear transformation.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(T(x)=x\) if and only if \(x\) is in \(W\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(T(x)=0\) if and only if \(x\) is in \(W^\perp\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(T\circ T = T\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. The range of \(T\) is \(W\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. We have to verify the Definition 1 of Section 3.3. Let \(x,y\) be vectors in \(\R^n\text{,}\) and let \(x = x_W + x_{W^\perp}\) and \(y = y_W + y_{W^\perp}\) be their orthogonal decompositions. Since \(W\) and \(W^\perp\) are subspaces, the sums \(x_W+y_W\) and \(x_{W^\perp}+y_{W^\perp}\) are in \(W\) and \(W^\perp\text{,}\) respectively. Therefore, the orthogonal decomposition of \(x+y\) is \((x_W+y_W)+(x_{W^\perp}+y_{W^\perp})\text{,}\) so
      \begin{gather*} T(x+y) = (x+y)_W = x_W+y_W = T(x) + T(y)\text{.} \end{gather*}
      Now let \(c\) be a scalar. Then \(cx_W\) is in \(W\) and \(cx_{W^\perp}\) is in \(W^\perp\text{,}\) so the orthogonal decomposition of \(cx\) is \(cx_W + cx_{W^\perp}\text{,}\) and therefore,
      \begin{gather*} T(cx) = (cx)_W = cx_W = cT(x)\text{.} \end{gather*}
      Since \(T\) satisfies the two Definition 1 of Section 3.3, it is a linear transformation.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. See Example 6.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. See Example 7.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. For any \(x\) in \(\R^n\) the vector \(T(x)\) is in \(W\text{,}\) so \(T\circ T(x) = T(T(x)) = T(x)\) by 2.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Any vector \(x\) in \(W\) is in the range of \(T\text{,}\) because \(T(x) = x\) for such vectors. On the other hand, for any vector \(x\) in \(\R^n\) the output \(T(x) = x_W\) is in \(W\text{,}\) so \(W\) is the range of \(T\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We compute the standard matrix of the orthogonal projection in the same way as for any other transformation: by evaluating on the standard coordinate vectors. In this case, this means projecting the standard coordinate vectors onto the subspace.
    🔗

    Example 6.3.32 Matrix of a projection.

    Let \(L\) be the line in \(\R^2\) spanned by the vector \(u = {3\choose 2}\text{,}\) and define \(T\colon\R^2\to\R^2\) by \(T(x)=x_L\text{.}\) Compute the standard matrix \(B\) for \(T\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    The columns of \(B\) are \(T(e_1) = (e_1)_L\) and \(T(e_2) = (e_2)_L\text{.}\) We have
    \begin{align*} \left. (e_1)_L \amp= \frac{u\cdot e_1}{u\cdot u}\,u = \frac{3}{13}\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\\ (e_2)_L \amp= \frac{u\cdot e_2}{u\cdot u}\,u = \frac{2}{13}\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
    \right\} \quad\implies\quad B = \frac 1{13}\begin{pmatrix} 9 \amp 6 \\ 6 \amp 4 \end{pmatrix}.
    🔗

    Example 6.3.34 Matrix of a projection.

    Continuing with Example 24, let
    \begin{gather*} W = \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{,} \end{gather*}
    and define \(T\colon\R^3\to\R^3\) by \(T(x)=x_W\text{.}\) Compute the standard matrix \(B\) for \(T\text{.}\)

    Solution.

    The columns of \(B\) are \(T(e_1) = (e_1)_W\text{,}\) \(T(e_2) = (e_2)_W\text{,}\) and \(T(e_3) = (e_3)_W\text{.}\) Let
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    To compute each \((e_i)_W\text{,}\) we solve the matrix equation \(A^TAc = A^Te_i\) for \(c\text{,}\) then use the equality \((e_i)_W = Ac\text{.}\) First we note that
    \begin{align*} A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix}; \qquad A^Te_i = \text{the $i$th column of } A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    For \(e_1\text{,}\) we form an augmented matrix and row reduce:
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 2 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \end{array}\right) \rref \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 \amp 0 \amp 1/3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1/3 \end{array}\right) \implies (e_1)_W = A\begin{pmatrix} 1/3 \\ 1/3 \end{pmatrix} = \frac 13\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}

    and for \(e_3\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 2 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \end{array}\right) \rref \left(\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 \amp 0 \amp -2/3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1/3 \end{array}\right) \implies (e_1)_W = A\begin{pmatrix} -2/3 \\ 1/3 \end{pmatrix} = \frac 13\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}

    \begin{align*} B = \frac 13\begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 1 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}

    Let \(W\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\) with basis \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\text{,}\) and let \(A\) be the matrix with columns \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\text{.}\) Then the standard matrix for \(T(x) = x_W\) is

    Proposition 6.3.35 Properties of Projection Matrices.

    Let \(W\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\text{,}\) define \(T\colon\R^n\to\R^n\) by \(T(x) = x_W\text{,}\) and let \(B\) be the standard matrix for \(T\text{.}\) Then:
    1. \(\displaystyle \Col(B) = W.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \Nul(B) = W^\perp.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle B^2 = B.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. If \(W \neq \{0\}\text{,}\) then 1 is an eigenvalue of \(B\) and the 1-eigenspace for \(B\) is \(W\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. If \(W \neq \R^n\text{,}\) then 0 is an eigenvalue of \(B\) and the 0-eigenspace for \(B\) is \(W^\perp\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    6. \(B\) is similar to the diagonal matrix with \(m\) ones and \(n-m\) zeros on the diagonal, where \(m = \dim(W).\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    The first four assertions are translations of Proposition 31, respectively, using Insight 23 of Section 3.1 and Theorem 25 of Section 3.4. The fifth assertion is equivalent to the second, by Fact 30 of Section 5.1.
    🔗
    For the final assertion, we showed in the proof of Theorem 2 that there is a basis of \(\R^n\) of the form \(\{v_1,\ldots,v_m,v_{m+1},\ldots,v_n\}\text{,}\) where \(\{v_1,\ldots,v_m\}\) is a basis for \(W\) and \(\{v_{m+1},\ldots,v_n\}\) is a basis for \(W^\perp\text{.}\) Each \(v_i\) is an eigenvector of \(B\text{:}\) indeed, for \(i\leq m\) we have
    \begin{gather*} Bv_i = T(v_i) = v_i = 1\cdot v_i \end{gather*}
    because \(v_i\) is in \(W\text{,}\) and for \(i > m\) we have
    \begin{gather*} Bv_i = T(v_i) = 0 = 0\cdot v_i \end{gather*}
    because \(v_i\) is in \(W^\perp\text{.}\) Therefore, we have found a basis of eigenvectors, with associated eigenvalues \(1,\ldots,1,0,\ldots,0\) (\(m\) ones and \(n-m\) zeros). Now we use the Theorem 11 of Section 5.3.
    🔗
    🔗
    We emphasize that the Proposition 35 would be very hard to prove in terms of matrices. By translating all of the statements into statements about linear transformations, they become much more transparent. For example, consider the projection matrix we found in Example 34. Just by looking at the matrix it is not at all obvious that when you square the matrix you get the same matrix back.

    Remark 6.3.37.

    As we saw in Example 36, if you are willing to compute bases for \(W\) and \(W^\perp\text{,}\) then this provides a third way of finding the standard matrix \(B\) for projection onto \(W\text{:}\) indeed, if \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\) is a basis for \(W\) and \(\{v_{m+1},v_{m+2},\ldots,v_n\}\) is a basis for \(W^\perp\text{,}\) then
    \begin{align*} B = \begin{pmatrix} | \amp | \amp | \\ v_1 \amp v_1 \amp \cdots \amp v_n \\ | \amp | \amp | \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp \cdots \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp \cdots \amp 0 \\ \vdots \amp \ddots \amp \vdots \amp \vdots \amp \ddots \amp \vdots \\ 0 \amp \cdots \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp \cdots \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \cdots \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp \cdots \amp 0 \\ \vdots \amp \ddots \amp \vdots \amp \vdots \amp \ddots \amp \vdots \\ 0 \amp \cdots \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp \cdots \amp 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} | \amp | \amp | \\ v_1 \amp v_1 \amp \cdots \amp v_n \\ | \amp | \amp | \end{pmatrix}\inv\text{,} \end{align*}
    where the middle matrix in the product is the diagonal matrix with \(m\) ones and \(n-m\) zeros on the diagonal. However, since you already have a basis for \(W\text{,}\) it is faster to multiply out the expression \(A(A^TA)\inv A^T\) as in the Corollary 29.
    🔗

    Remark 6.3.38 Reflections.

    Let \(W\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\text{,}\) and let \(x\) be a vector in \(\R^n\text{.}\) The reflection of \(x\) over \(W\) is defined to be the vector
    \begin{gather*} \refl_W(x) = x - 2x_{W^\perp}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    In other words, to find \(\refl_W(x)\) one starts at \(x\text{,}\) then moves to \(x-x_{W^\perp} = x_W\text{,}\) then continues in the same direction one more time, to end on the opposite side of \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    🔗
    Since \(x_{W^\perp} = x - x_W\text{,}\) we also have
    \begin{gather*} \refl_W(x) = x - 2(x - x_W) = 2x_W - x\text{.} \end{gather*}
    We leave it to the reader to check using the definition that:
    1. \(\displaystyle \refl_W\circ\refl_W = \Id_{\R^n}.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The \(1\)-eigenspace of \(\refl_W\) is \(W\text{,}\) and the \(-1\)-eigenspace of \(\refl_W\) is \(W^\perp\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\refl_W\) is similar to the diagonal matrix with \(m = \dim(W)\) ones on the diagonal and \(n-m\) negative ones.
      🔗
      🔗

    10.

    True or false. Justify your answer! Suppose that \(P: {\bf R}^2 \to {\bf R}^2\) and \(Q: {\bf R}^2 \to {\bf R}^2\) are orthogonal projections. Then the product \(PQ\) must be an orthogonal projection.
    🔗
    Solution.
    False. If we take \(P\) to be projection onto the \(x\)-axis and \(Q\) to be projection onto the line \(y=x\text{,}\) then
    \begin{align*} PQ \amp = \begin{pmatrix}1 \amp 0\\ 0 \amp 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1/2 \amp 1/2\\ 1/2 \amp 1/2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1/2 \amp 1/2\\ 0 \amp 0\end{pmatrix}\\ (PQ)^2 \amp = \begin{pmatrix}1/2 \amp 1/2\\ 0 \amp 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1/2 \amp 1/2\\ 0 \amp 0\end{pmatrix}= \begin{pmatrix}1/4 \amp 1/4\\ 0 \amp 0\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    \(PQ\) is not the same as \((PQ)^2\text{,}\) so \(PQ\) is not a projection.
    🔗
    This means that if we take the composition of linear transformations that are orthogonal projections onto different subspaces, then the composition is not necessarily an orthogonal projection.

    11.

    True or false. Justify your answer! Suppose that \(P: {\bf R}^n \to {\bf R}^n\) is an orthogonal projections. Then \(I-P\) is an orthogonal projection.
    🔗
    Solution.
    True. If \(P\) is the orthogonal projection onto a subspace \(W\text{,}\) then \(I-P\) is the orthogonal projection onto \(W^{\perp}\) since \(I-P\) destroys every vector in \(W\) and fixes every vector in \(W^{\perp}\text{:}\) if \(w\) is in \(W\) then \((I-P)(w) = w - Pw = w - w = 0\text{,}\) and if \(y\) is in \(W^{\perp}\) then \((I-P)(y) = y - Py = y - 0 = y\text{.}\) Alternatively, we could use the properties \(P^T = P\) and \(P^2 = P\) to verify that \(I-P\) is an orthogonal projection abstractly through the fact that \((I-P)^T = I^T-P^T = I-P\) and
    \begin{gather*} (I-P)^2 = (I-P)(I-P) = I^2 - IP - PI +P^2 = I-P-P+P = I-P\text{.} \end{gather*}
