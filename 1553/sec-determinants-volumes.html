    Section 4.3 Determinants and Volumes

    Objectives

    1. Understand the relationship between the determinant of a matrix and the volume of a parallelepiped.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Learn to use determinants to compute volumes of parallelograms and triangles.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Learn to use determinants to compute the volume of some curvy shapes like ellipses.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Pictures: parallelepiped, the image of a curvy shape under a linear transformation.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Theorem: determinants and volumes.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Vocabulary word: parallelepiped.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    In this section we give a geometric interpretation of determinants, in terms of volumes. This will shed light on the reason behind three of the four defining properties of the determinant. It is also a crucial ingredient in the change-of-variables formula in multivariable calculus.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.3.1 Parallelograms and Paralellepipeds

    The determinant computes the volume of the following kind of geometric object.
    🔗

    Definition 4.3.1.

    The paralellepiped determined by \(n\) vectors \(v_1, v_2,\ldots,v_n\) in \(\R^n\) is the subset
    \begin{gather*} P = \bigl\{a_1x_1 + a_2x_2 + \cdots + a_nx_n \bigm| 0 \leq a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n\leq 1\bigr\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    In other words, a parallelepiped is the set of all linear combinations of \(n\) vectors with coefficients in \([0,1]\text{.}\) We can draw parallelepipeds using the parallelogram law for vector addition.
    🔗

    Example 4.3.2 The unit cube.

    The parallelepiped determined by the standard coordinate vectors \(e_1,e_2,\ldots,e_n\) is the unit \(n\)-dimensional cube. \usetikzlibrary{math}
    🔗
    
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.3.3 Parallelograms.

    When \(n = 2\text{,}\) a paralellepiped is just a paralellogram in \(\R^2\text{.}\) Note that the edges come in parallel pairs.
    🔗
    
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.3.4.

    When \(n=3\text{,}\) a parallelepiped is a kind of a skewed cube. Note that the faces come in parallel pairs.
    🔗
    
    🔗
    🔗
    When does a parallelepiped have zero volume? This can happen only if the parallelepiped is flat, i.e., it is squashed into a lower dimension.
    🔗
    
    🔗
    This means exactly that \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\}\) is linearly dependent, which by Corollary 16 of Section 4.1 means that the matrix with rows \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\) has determinant zero. To summarize:
    🔗

    Note 4.3.5.

    The parallelepiped defined by \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\) has zero volume if and only if the matrix with rows \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\) has zero determinant.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.3.2 Determinants and Volumes

    The key observation above is only the beginning of the story: the volume of a parallelepiped is always a determinant.
    🔗

    Theorem 4.3.6 Determinants and volumes.

    Let \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\) be vectors in \(\R^n\text{,}\) let \(P\) be the parallelepiped determined by these vectors, and let \(A\) be the matrix with rows \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\text{.}\) Then the absolute value of the determinant of \(A\) is the volume of \(P\text{:}\)
    \begin{gather*} |\det(A)| = \vol(P)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Since the four defining properties characterize the determinant, they also characterize the absolute value of the determinant. Explicitly, \(|\det|\) is a function on square matrices which satisfies these properties:
    1. Doing a row replacement on \(A\) does not change \(|\det(A)|\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Scaling a row of \(A\) by a scalar \(c\) multiplies \(|\det(A)|\) by \(|c|\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Swapping two rows of a matrix does not change \(|\det(A)|\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. The determinant of the identity matrix \(I_n\) is equal to \(1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    The absolute value of the determinant is the only such function: indeed, by Recipe 5 of Section 4.1, if you do some number of row operations on \(A\) to obtain a matrix \(B\) in row echelon form, then
    \begin{gather*} |\det(A)| = \left|\frac{\text{(product of the diagonal entries of $B$)}} {\text{(product of scaling factors used)}}\right|\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    For a square matrix \(A\text{,}\) we abuse notation and let \(\vol(A)\) denote the volume of the paralellepiped determined by the rows of \(A\text{.}\) Then we can regard \(\vol\) as a function from the set of square matrices to the real numbers. We will show that \(\vol\) also satisfies the above four properties.
    1. For simplicity, we consider a row replacement of the form \(R_n = R_n + cR_i\text{.}\) The volume of a paralellepiped is the volume of its base, times its height: here the “base” is the paralellepiped determined by \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_{n-1}\text{,}\) and the “height” is the perpendicular distance of \(v_n\) from the base.
      🔗
      
      🔗
      Translating \(v_n\) by a multiple of \(v_i\) moves \(v_n\) in a direction parallel to the base. This changes neither the base nor the height! Thus, \(\vol(A)\) is unchanged by row replacements. \usetikzlibrary{math}
      🔗
      
      🔗
      🔗
    2. For simplicity, we consider a row scale of the form \(R_n = cR_n.\) This scales the length of \(v_n\) by a factor of \(|c|\text{,}\) which also scales the perpendicular distance of \(v_n\) from the base by a factor of \(|c|\text{.}\) Thus, \(\vol(A)\) is scaled by \(|c|\text{.}\) \usetikzlibrary{math}
      🔗
      
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Swapping two rows of \(A\) just reorders the vectors \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\text{,}\) hence has no effect on the parallelepiped determined by those vectors. Thus, \(\vol(A)\) is unchanged by row swaps.
      🔗
      
      🔗
      🔗
    4. The rows of the identity matrix \(I_n\) are the standard coordinate vectors \(e_1,e_2,\ldots,e_n\text{.}\) The associated paralellepiped is the unit cube, which has volume \(1\text{.}\) Thus, \(\vol(I_n) = 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Since \(|\det|\) is the only function satisfying these properties, we have
    \begin{gather*} \vol(P) = \vol(A) = |\det(A)|\text{.} \end{gather*}
    This completes the proof.
    🔗
    🔗
    Since \(\det(A) = \det(A^T)\) by the transpose property, the absolute value of \(\det(A)\) is also equal to the volume of the paralellepiped determined by the columns of \(A\) as well.
    🔗

    Example 4.3.7 Length.

    A \(1\times 1\) matrix \(A\) is just a number \(\mat a\text{.}\) In this case, the parallelepiped \(P\) determined by its one row is just the interval \([0,a]\) (or \([a,0]\) if \(a\lt0\)). The “volume” of a region in \(\R^1 = \R\) is just its length, so it is clear in this case that \(\vol(P) = |a|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.3.8 Area.

    When \(A\) is a \(2\times 2\) matrix, its rows determine a parallelogram in \(\R^2\text{.}\) The “volume” of a region in \(\R^2\) is its area, so we obtain a formula for the area of a parallelogram: it is the determinant of the matrix whose rows are the vectors forming two adjacent sides of the parallelogram.
    🔗
    
    🔗
    It is perhaps surprising that it is possible to compute the area of a parallelogram without trigonometry. It is a fun geometry problem to prove this formula by hand. [Hint: first think about the case when the first row of \(A\) lies on the \(x\)-axis.]
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.3.9.

    Find the area of the parallelogram with sides \((1,3)\) and \((2,-3)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    🔗

    Solution.

    The area is
    \begin{align*} \left|\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp -3 \end{pmatrix}\right| = |-3-6| = 9\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.3.10.

    Find the area of the parallelogram in the picture.
    🔗
    
    🔗

    Solution.

    We choose two adjacent sides to be the rows of a matrix. We choose the top two:
    🔗
    
    🔗
    Note that we do not need to know where the origin is in the picture: vectors are determined by their length and direction, not where they start. The area is
    \begin{align*} \left|\det\begin{pmatrix} -1 \amp -4 \\ 2 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\right| = |1+8| = 9\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.3.11 Area of a triangle.

    Find the area of the triangle with vertices \((-1,-2), \,(2,-1),\,(1,3).\)
    🔗
    
    🔗

    Solution.

    Doubling a triangle makes a paralellogram. We choose two of its sides to be the rows of a matrix.
    🔗
    
    🔗
    The area of the parallelogram is
    \begin{align*} \left|\det\begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp 5 \\ 3 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\right| = |2-15| = 13\text{,} \end{align*}
    so the area of the triangle is \(13/2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    You might be wondering: if the absolute value of the determinant is a volume, what is the geometric meaning of the determinant without the absolute value? The next remark explains that we can think of the determinant as a signed volume. If you have taken an integral calculus course, you probably computed negative areas under curves; the idea here is similar.
    🔗

    Remark 4.3.12 Signed volumes.

    The Theorem 6 on determinants and volumes tells us that the absolute value of the determinant is the volume of a paralellepiped. This raises the question of whether the sign of the determinant has any geometric meaning.
    🔗
    A \(1\times 1\) matrix \(A\) is just a number \(\mat a\text{.}\) In this case, the parallelepiped \(P\) determined by its one row is just the interval \([0,a]\) if \(a \geq 0\text{,}\) and it is \([a,0]\) if \(a\lt0\text{.}\) In this case, the sign of the determinant determines whether the interval is to the left or the right of the origin.
    🔗
    For a \(2\times 2\) matrix with rows \(v_1,v_2\text{,}\) the sign of the determinant determines whether \(v_2\) is counterclockwise or clockwise from \(v_1\text{.}\) That is, if the counterclockwise angle from \(v_1\) to \(v_2\) is less than \(180^\circ\text{,}\) then the determinant is positive; otherwise it is negative (or zero). \usetikzlibrary{angles} \begin{tikzpicture}[thin border nodes, scale=1.3] \filldraw[fill=seq-orange, fill opacity=.2, very thin] (0,0) -- (1,2) -- (3,3) -- (2,1) -- cycle; \draw[vector] (0,0) -- node[auto]{$v_2$} (1,2); \draw[vector] (0,0) -- node[auto,swap]{$v_1$} (2,1); \coordinate (o) at (0,0); \coordinate (v1) at (2,1); \coordinate (v2) at (1,2); \pic[draw, ->, angle radius=1cm] {angle=v1--o--v2}; \node at (1.5,-.7) {$\det\begin{pmatrix} \matrow{v_1} \\ \matrow{v_2} \end{pmatrix} > 0$}; \end{tikzpicture} \qquad \begin{tikzpicture}[thin border nodes, scale=1.3] \filldraw[fill=seq-orange, fill opacity=.2, very thin] (0,0) -- (1,2) -- (3,3) -- (2,1) -- cycle; \draw[vector] (0,0) -- node[auto]{$v_1$} (1,2); \draw[vector] (0,0) -- node[auto,swap]{$v_2$} (2,1); \coordinate (o) at (0,0); \coordinate (v1) at (2,1); \coordinate (v2) at (1,2); \pic[draw, <-, angle radius=1cm] {angle=v1--o--v2}; \node at (1.5,-.7) {$\det\begin{pmatrix} \matrow{v_1} \\ \matrow{v_2} \end{pmatrix} < 0$}; \end{tikzpicture} For example, if \(v_1 = {a\choose b}\text{,}\) then the counterclockwise rotation of \(v_1\) by \(90^\circ\) is \(v_2 = {-b\choose a}\) by Example 18 of Section 3.3, and
    \begin{align*} \det\begin{pmatrix} a \amp b \\ -b \amp a \end{pmatrix} = a^2 + b^2 > 0\text{.} \end{align*}
    On the other hand, the clockwise rotation of \(v_1\) by \(90^\circ\) is \(b\choose -a\text{,}\) and
    \begin{align*} \det\begin{pmatrix} a \amp b \\ b \amp -a \end{pmatrix} = -a^2 - b^2 \lt 0\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    For a \(3\times 3\) matrix with rows \(v_1,v_2,v_3\text{,}\) the right-hand rule determines the sign of the determinant. If you point the index finger of your right hand in the direction of \(v_1\) and your middle finger in the direction of \(v_2\text{,}\) then the determinant is positive if your thumb points roughly in the direction of \(v_3\text{,}\) and it is negative otherwise.
    🔗
    
    🔗
    In higher dimensions, the notion of signed volume is still important, but it is usually defined in terms of the sign of a determinant.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.3.3 Volumes of Regions

    Let \(A\) be an \(n\times n\) matrix with columns \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\text{,}\) and let \(T\colon\R^n\to\R^n\) be the associated matrix transformation \(T(x)=Ax\text{.}\) Then \(T(e_1)=v_1\) and \(T(e_2)=v_2\text{,}\) so \(T\) takes the unit cube \(C\) to the parallelepiped \(P\) determined by \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\text{:}\)
    🔗
    
    🔗
    Since the unit cube has volume \(1\) and its image has volume \(|\det(A)|\text{,}\) the transformation \(T\) scaled the volume of the cube by a factor of \(|\det(A)|\text{.}\) To rephrase:
    🔗
    If \(A\) is an \(n\times n\) matrix with corresponding matrix transformation \(T\colon\R^n\to\R^n\text{,}\) and if \(C\) is the unit cube in \(\R^n\text{,}\) then the volume of \(T(C)\) is \(|\det(A)|.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The notation \(T(S)\) means the image of the region \(S\) under the transformation \(T\text{.}\) In set builder notation, this is the subset
    \begin{gather*} T(S) = \bigl\{T(x)\mid x \text{ in } S\bigr\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    In fact, \(T\) scales the volume of any region in \(\R^n\) by the same factor, even for curvy regions.
    🔗

    Theorem 4.3.13.

    Let \(A\) be an \(n\times n\) matrix, and let \(T\colon\R^n\to\R^n\) be the associated matrix transformation \(T(x)=Ax\text{.}\) If \(S\) is any region in \(\R^n\text{,}\) then
    \begin{gather*} \vol(T(S))=|\det(A)|\cdot\vol(S)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(C\) be the unit cube, let \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\) be the columns of \(A\text{,}\) and let \(P\) be the paralellepiped determined by these vectors, so \(T(C) = P\) and \(\vol(P) = |\det(A)|\text{.}\) For \(\epsilon > 0\) we let \(\epsilon C\) be the cube with side lengths \(\epsilon\text{,}\) i.e., the paralellepiped determined by the vectors \(\epsilon e_1,\epsilon e_2,\ldots,\epsilon e_n\text{,}\) and we define \(\epsilon P\) similarly. By the second defining property, \(T\) takes \(\epsilon C\) to \(\epsilon P\text{.}\) The volume of \(\epsilon C\) is \(\epsilon^n\) (we scaled each of the \(n\) standard vectors by a factor of \(\epsilon\)) and the volume of \(\epsilon P\) is \(\epsilon^n|\det(A)|\) (for the same reason), so we have shown that \(T\) scales the volume of \(\epsilon C\) by \(|\det(A)|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    🔗
    By the first defining property, the image of a translate of \(\epsilon C\) is a translate of \(\epsilon P\text{:}\)
    \begin{gather*} T(x + \epsilon C) = T(x) + \epsilon T(C) = T(x) + \epsilon P\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Since a translation does not change volumes, this proves that \(T\) scales the volume of a translate of \(\epsilon C\) by \(|\det(A)|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    At this point, we need to use techniques from multivariable calculus, so we only give an idea of the rest of the proof. Any region \(S\) can be approximated by a collection of very small cubes of the form \(x + \epsilon C\text{.}\) The image \(T(S)\) is then approximated by the image of this collection of cubes, which is a collection of very small paralellepipeds of the form \(T(x) + \epsilon P\text{.}\) \def\curvy{(-37.3333, 2.6667) .. controls (-37.3333, -5.3333) and (-26.6667, -10.6667) ..     Example 4.3.14.

    Let \(S\) be a half-circle of radius \(1\text{,}\) let
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{,} \end{align*}
    and define \(T\colon\R^2\to\R^2\) by \(T(x)=Ax\text{.}\) What is the area of \(T(S)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    
    🔗

    Solution.

    The area of the unit circle is \(\pi\text{,}\) so the area of \(S\) is \(\pi/2\text{.}\) The transformation \(T\) scales areas by a factor of \(|\det(A)| = |1 - 4| = 3\text{,}\) so
    \begin{gather*} \vol(T(S)) = 3\vol(S) = \frac{3\pi}2\text{.} \end{gather*}

    Example 4.3.15 Area of an ellipse.

    Find the area of the interior \(E\) of the ellipse defined by the equation
    \begin{gather*} \left(\frac{2x-y}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{y+3x}{3}\right)^2 = 1\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    This ellipse is obtained from the unit circle \(X^2+Y^2=1\) by the linear change of coordinates
    \begin{align*} X \amp= \frac{2x-y}2\\ Y \amp= \frac{y+3x}3\text{.} \end{align*}
    In other words, if we define a linear transformation \(T\colon\R^2\to\R^2\) by
    \begin{gather*} T\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} (2x-y)/2 \\ (y+3x)/3 \end{pmatrix}\text{,} \end{gather*}
    then \(T{x\choose y}\) lies on the unit circle \(C\) whenever \(x\choose y\) lies on \(E\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    🔗
    We compute the standard matrix \(A\) for \(T\) by evaluating on the standard coordinate vectors:
    \begin{align*} T\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \qquad T\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1/2 \\ 1/3 \end{pmatrix} \quad\implies\quad A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -1/2 \\ 1 \amp 1/3 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Therefore, \(T\) scales areas by a factor of \(|\det(A)| = |\frac13+\frac12| = \frac56.\) The area of the unit circle is \(\pi\text{,}\) so
    \begin{gather*} \pi = \vol(C) = \vol(T(E)) = |\det(A)|\cdot\vol(E) = \frac56\vol(E)\text{,} \end{gather*}
    and thus the area of \(E\) is \(6\pi/5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.3.16 Multiplicativity of \(|\det|\,\).

    The above Theorem 13 also gives a geometric reason for multiplicativity of the (absolute value of the) determinant. Indeed, let \(A\) and \(B\) be \(n\times n\) matrices, and let \(T,U\colon\R^n\to\R^n\) be the corresponding matrix transformations. If \(C\) is the unit cube, then
    \begin{align*} \vol\bigl(T\circ U(C)\bigr) = \vol\bigl(T(U(C))\bigr) \amp= |\det(A)|\vol(U(C))\\ \amp= |\det(A)|\cdot|\det(B)|\vol(C)\\ \amp= |\det(A)|\cdot|\det(B)|\text{.} \end{align*}
    On the other hand, the matrix for the composition \(T\circ U\) is the product \(AB\text{,}\) so
    \begin{gather*} \vol\bigl(T\circ U(C)\bigr) = |\det(AB)|\vol(C) = |\det(AB)|\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Thus \(|\det(AB)| = |\det(A)|\cdot|\det(B)|\text{.}\)

    4.

    Find the area of the triangle with vertices
    \begin{gather*} (4,-5), \quad (1, 2), \quad (-10, -3)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The area is half of the area of the corresponding parallelogram. To use the formula from section 4.2, we need to find vectors \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) that determine the triangle. For this, we can start at any vertex (we will use the first vertex) and find the vectors to each of its two adjacent vertices within the triangle.
    🔗
    The vector from \((4, -5)\) to \((1, 2)\) is \(v_1 = \begin{pmatrix}-3 \\ 7\end{pmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The vector from \((4, -5)\) to \((-10, -3)\) is \(v_2 = \begin{pmatrix}-14 \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\text{.}\)

    7.

    Find all values of \(h\) so that the volume of the parallelepiped \(P\) formed by the four vectors below is equal to \(15\text{.}\)
    \begin{gather*} v_1 = \begin{pmatrix}-2 \\ 0 \\ h \\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad v_2 = \begin{pmatrix}4 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad v_3 = \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 2 \\ 1 \\ 9\end{pmatrix}, \qquad v_4 = \begin{pmatrix}6 \\ 4 \\ 5 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    There are two values of \(h\) that work: \(h = 5/82\) and \(h = 35/82\text{.}\) The volume of \(P\) is the absolute value of the determinant of the matrix with columns \(v_1\) through \(v_4\text{,}\) so we set it equal to \(15\) and solve for \(h\text{.}\) We use the cofactor expansion along the first column.
    \begin{align*} 15 \amp = \left|\det\begin{pmatrix}-2 \amp 4 \amp 0 \amp 6\\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \amp 4\\ h \amp 1 \amp 1 \amp 5\\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 9 \amp 1\end{pmatrix}\right| = \left|-2C_{11} + 0 C_{21} + h C_{31} + 0 C_{41}\right|\\ \amp = \left|-2(-1)^{1+1} \det\begin{pmatrix}1 \amp 2 \amp 4\\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 5\\ 0 \amp 9 \amp 1\end{pmatrix} + h(-1)^{3+1} \det\begin{pmatrix}4 \amp 0 \amp 6\\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 4\\ 0 \amp 9 \amp 1\end{pmatrix}\right|\\ \amp = \left| -2 \Big[1(1-9\cdot 5)-2(1-0)+4(9-0)\Big] + h \Big[4(2\cdot 1-9 \cdot 4)-0+6(1 \cdot 9 -0)\Big] \right|\\ \amp = \left| -2(-44-2+36) + h(-136+54)\right|\\ \amp = \left|-2(-10) - 82h\right| = \left|20-82h\right|\text{.} \end{align*}
    Therefore, \(\left|20-82h\right| = 15\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Case 1: \(20-82h = 15\text{,}\) so \(82h = 5\) and \(h = 5/82\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Case 2: \(-(20-82h) = 15\text{,}\) so \(-20+82h = 15\text{,}\) thus \(82h = 35\) and \(h = 35/82\text{.}\)

    8.

    Let \(R\) be the triangle with vertices \((0, 0)\text{,}\) \((1, 4)\text{,}\) and \((10, 20)\text{.}\) Find the area of the new triangle \(T(R)\text{,}\) where \(T: {\bf R}^2 \to {\bf R}^2\) is the transformation \(T(x) = \begin{pmatrix}8 \amp -3 \\ 1 \amp 2\end{pmatrix}\text{.}\)

    Solution.
    The area of \(R\) is
    \begin{align*} \dfrac{1}{2} \left| \det\begin{pmatrix}1 \amp 10 \\ 4 \amp 20\end{pmatrix}\right| = \dfrac{1}{2}|-20| = 10\text{.} \end{align*}

    \begin{gather*} \textup{Area}(T(R)) = \left|\det(A)\right|\cdot \textup{Area}(R) = 19(10) = 190\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Another way to do this problem is to transform the vertices of the original triangle into the new triangle: we compute \(T\begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 0\end{pmatrix}\text{,}\)

    Therefore, the vertices of \(T(R)\) are \((0, 0)\text{,}\) \((-4,9)\text{,}\) and \((20,50)\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} \textup{Area}(T(R)) = \dfrac{1}{2} \left|\det\begin{pmatrix}-4 \amp 20 \\ 9 \amp 50\end{pmatrix}\right| = \dfrac{1}{2} |-200-180| = \dfrac{1}{2}(380) = 190\text{.} \end{align*}

    11.

    Suppose \(u\) and \(v\) are vectors in \({\bf R}^2\) that naturally form a parallelogram with area \(10\) square units. What is the area of the parallelogram naturally formed by \(3u\) and \(-v\text{.}\) Justify this answer in two ways: first by using geometry, then by using properties of determinants.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Geometrically: stretching \(u\) by a factor of three will scale the area by a factor of \(3\text{,}\) whereas multiplying \(v\) by \(-1\) will only change the direction of facing of the parallelogram. Therefore, the resulting area is \(3(10)=30\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Determinants: the area of the parallelogram determined by \(u\) and \(v\) is
    \begin{align*} \textup{Area} = \left|\det \begin{pmatrix}- \amp u \amp - \\ - \amp v \amp -\end{pmatrix}\right|=10 \textup{ units}^2\text{.} \end{align*}
    Scaling a row of a matrix by \(3\) multiplies the determinant by \(3\text{,}\) and multiplying a row by \(-1\) changes the determinant by \(-1\) (this will be irrelevant since we take the absolute value).
    \begin{align*} \textup{Area} = \left|\det \begin{pmatrix}- \amp 3u \amp - \\ - \amp -v \amp -\end{pmatrix}\right| = |10(3)(-1)| = 30 \textup{ units}^2\text{.} \end{align*}
