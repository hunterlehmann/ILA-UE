Proposition 3.5.26.

  1. A transformation \(T\colon\R^n\to\R^n\) is invertible if and only if it is both one-to-one and onto.
    🔗
    🔗
  2. If \(T\) is already known to be invertible, then \(U\colon\R^n\to\R^n\) is the inverse of \(T\) provided that either \(T\circ U = \Id_{\R^n}\) or \(U\circ T = \Id_{\R^n}\text{:}\) it is only necessary to verify one.
    🔗
    🔗
🔗
🔗

Proof.
To say that \(T\) is one-to-one and onto means that \(T(x)=b\) has exactly one solution for every \(b\) in \(\R^n\text{.}\)
🔗
Suppose that \(T\) is invertible. Then \(T(x)=b\) always has the unique solution \(x = T\inv(b)\text{:}\) indeed, applying \(T\inv\) to both sides of \(T(x)=b\) gives
\begin{gather*} x = T\inv(T(x)) = T\inv(b)\text{,} \end{gather*}
and applying \(T\) to both sides of \(x = T\inv(b)\) gives
\begin{gather*} T(x) = T(T\inv(b)) = b\text{.} \end{gather*}
🔗
Conversely, suppose that \(T\) is one-to-one and onto. Let \(b\) be a vector in \(\R^n\text{,}\) and let \(x = U(b)\) be the unique solution of \(T(x)=b\text{.}\) Then \(U\) defines a transformation from \(\R^n\) to \(\R^n\text{.}\) For any \(x\) in \(\R^n\text{,}\) we have \(U(T(x)) = x\text{,}\) because \(x\) is the unique solution of the equation \(T(x) = b\) for \(b = T(x)\text{.}\) For any \(b\) in \(\R^n\text{,}\) we have \(T(U(b)) = b\text{,}\) because \(x = U(b)\) is the unique solution of \(T(x)=b\text{.}\) Therefore, \(U\) is the inverse of \(T\text{,}\) and \(T\) is invertible.
🔗
Suppose now that \(T\) is an invertible transformation, and that \(U\) is another transformation such that \(T\circ U = \Id_{\R^n}\text{.}\) We must show that \(U = T\inv\text{,}\) i.e., that \(U\circ T = \Id_{\R^n}\text{.}\) We compose both sides of the equality \(T\circ U = \Id_{\R^n}\) on the left by \(T\inv\) and on the right by \(T\) to obtain
\begin{gather*} T\inv\circ T\circ U\circ T = T\inv\circ\Id_{\R^n}\circ T\text{.} \end{gather*}
We have \(T\inv\circ T = \Id_{\R^n}\) and \(\Id_{\R^n}\circ U = U\text{,}\) so the left side of the above equation is \(U\circ T\text{.}\) Likewise, \(\Id_{\R^n}\circ T = T\) and \(T\inv\circ T = \Id_{\R^n}\text{,}\) so our equality simplifies to \(U\circ T = \Id_{\R^n}\text{,}\) as desired.
🔗
If instead we had assumed only that \(U\circ T = \Id_{\R^n}\text{,}\) then the proof that \(T\circ U = \Id_{\R^n}\) proceeds similarly.
