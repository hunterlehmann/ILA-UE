Theorem 5.3.52 Diagonalization Theorem, Variant.

Let \(A\) be an \(n\times n\) matrix. The following are equivalent:
  1. \(A\) is diagonalizable.
    🔗
    🔗
  2. The sum of the geometric multiplicities of the eigenvalues of \(A\) is equal to \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  3. The sum of the algebraic multiplicities of the eigenvalues of \(A\) is equal to \(n\text{,}\) and for each eigenvalue, the geometric multiplicity equals the algebraic multiplicity.
    🔗
    🔗
🔗
🔗

Proof.
We will show \(1\implies 2\implies 3\implies 1\text{.}\) First suppose that \(A\) is diagonalizable. Then \(A\) has \(n\) linearly independent eigenvectors \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\text{.}\) This implies that the sum of the geometric multiplicities is at least \(n\text{:}\) for instance, if \(v_1,v_2,v_3\) have the same eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{,}\) then the geometric multiplicity of \(\lambda\) is at least \(3\) (as the \(\lambda\)-eigenspace contains three linearly independent vectors), and so on. But the sum of the algebraic multiplicities is greater than or equal to the sum of the geometric multiplicities by the Theorem 50, and the sum of the algebraic multiplicities is at most \(n\) because the characteristic polynomial has degree \(n\text{.}\) Therefore, the sum of the geometric multiplicities equals \(n\text{.}\)
🔗
Now suppose that the sum of the geometric multiplicities equals \(n\text{.}\) As above, this forces the sum of the algebraic multiplicities to equal \(n\) as well. As the algebraic multiplicities are all greater than or equal to the geometric multiplicities in any case, this implies that they are in fact equal.
🔗
Finally, suppose that the third condition is satisfied. Then the sum of the geometric multiplicities equals \(n\text{.}\) Suppose that the distinct eigenvectors are \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\ldots,\lambda_k\text{,}\) and that \(\cB_i\) is a basis for the \(\lambda_i\)-eigenspace, which we call \(V_i\text{.}\) We claim that the collection \(\cB = \{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\}\) of all vectors in all of the eigenspace bases \(\cB_i\) is linearly independent. Consider the vector equation
\begin{gather*} 0 = c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_nv_n\text{.} \end{gather*}
Grouping the eigenvectors with the same eigenvalues, this sum has the form
\begin{gather*} 0 = \text{(something in $V_1$)} + \text{(something in $V_2$)} + \cdots + \text{(something in $V_k$)}\text{.} \end{gather*}
Since eigenvectors with distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent, each “something in \(V_i\)” is equal to zero. But this implies that all coefficients \(c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_n\) are equal to zero, since the vectors in each \(\cB_i\) are linearly independent. Therefore, \(A\) has \(n\) linearly independent eigenvectors, so it is diagonalizable.
