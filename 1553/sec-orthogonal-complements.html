    Section 6.2 Orthogonal Complements

    Objectives

    1. Understand the basic properties of orthogonal complements.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Learn to compute the orthogonal complement of a subspace.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Recipes: shortcuts for computing the orthogonal complements of common subspaces.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Picture: orthogonal complements in \(\R^2\) and \(\R^3\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Theorem: row rank equals column rank.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Vocabulary words: orthogonal complement, row space.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    It will be important to compute the set of all vectors that are orthogonal to a given set of vectors. It turns out that a vector is orthogonal to a set of vectors if and only if it is orthogonal to the span of those vectors, which is a subspace, so we restrict ourselves to the case of subspaces.
    🔗

    Subsection 6.2.1 Definition of the Orthogonal Complement

    Taking the orthogonal complement is an operation that is performed on subspaces.
    🔗

    Definition 6.2.1.

    Let \(W\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\text{.}\) Its orthogonal complement is the subspace
    \begin{gather*} W^\perp = \bigl\{ \text{$v$ in $\R^n$}\mid v\cdot w=0 \text{ for all $w$ in $W$} \bigr\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    The symbol \(W^\perp\) is sometimes read “\(W\) perp.”
    🔗
    🔗
    This is the set of all vectors \(v\) in \(\R^n\) that are orthogonal to all of the vectors in \(W\text{.}\) We will show Fact 16 that \(W^\perp\) is indeed a subspace.
    🔗

    Note 6.2.2.

    We now have two similar-looking pieces of notation:
    \begin{align*} A^{\mh{T}} \amp\text{ is the transpose of a matrix $A$}.\\ W^{\mh{\perp}} \amp\text{ is the orthogonal complement of a subspace $W$}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Try not to confuse the two.
    🔗
    🔗

    Pictures of orthogonal complements.

    The orthogonal complement of a line \(\mhb{W}\) through the origin in \(\R^2\) is the perpendicular line \(\mhg{W^\perp}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    🔗

    Interactive 6.2.3 Orthogonal complements in \(\R^2\).

    🔗
    The orthogonal complement of a line \(\mhb{W}\) in \(\R^3\) is the perpendicular plane \(\mhg{W^\perp}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    🔗

    Interactive 6.2.5 Orthogonal complements in \(\R^3\).

    🔗
    The orthogonal complement of a plane \(\mhb{W}\) in \(\R^3\) is the perpendicular line \(\mhg{W^\perp}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    🔗

    Interactive 6.2.7 Orthogonal complements in \(\R^3\).

    🔗
    We see in the above pictures that \((W^\perp)^\perp = W\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 6.2.9.

    The orthogonal complement of \(\R^n\) is \(\{0\}\text{,}\) since the zero vector is the only vector that is orthogonal to all of the vectors in \(\R^n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For the same reason, we have \(\{0\}^\perp = \R^n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.2.2 Computing Orthogonal Complements

    Since any subspace is a span, the following proposition gives a recipe for computing the orthogonal complement of any subspace. However, below we will give several shortcuts for computing the orthogonal complements of other common kinds of subspaces–in particular, null spaces. To compute the orthogonal complement of a general subspace, usually it is best to rewrite the subspace as the column space or null space of a matrix, as in Note .
    🔗

    Proposition 6.2.10 The orthogonal complement of a column space.

    Let \(A\) be a matrix and let \(W=\Col(A)\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{gather*} W^\perp = \Nul(A^T)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    To justify the first equality, we need to show that a vector \(x\) is perpendicular to the all of the vectors in \(W\) if and only if it is perpendicular only to \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\text{.}\) Since the \(v_i\) are contained in \(W\text{,}\) we really only have to show that if \(x\cdot v_1 = x\cdot v_2 = \cdots = x\cdot v_m = 0\text{,}\) then \(x\) is perpendicular to every vector \(v\) in \(W\text{.}\) Indeed, any vector in \(W\) has the form \(v = c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_mv_m\) for suitable scalars \(c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_m\text{,}\) so
    \begin{align*} x\cdot v \amp= x\cdot(c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_mv_m)\\ \amp= c_1(x\cdot v_1) + c_2(x\cdot v_2) + \cdots + c_m(x\cdot v_m)\\ \amp= c_1(0) + c_2(0) + \cdots + c_m(0) = 0\text{.} \end{align*}
    Therefore, \(x\) is in \(W^\perp.\)
    🔗
    To prove the second equality, we let
    \begin{gather*} A = \begin{pmatrix} \matrow{v_1^T} \\ \matrow{v_2^T} \\ \vdots \\ \matrow{v_m^T} \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    By the Definition 9 of Section 2.3, for any vector \(x\) in \(\R^n\) we have
    \begin{gather*} Ax = \begin{pmatrix} v_1^Tx \\ v_2^Tx \\ \vdots \\ v_m^Tx \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} v_1\cdot x \\ v_2\cdot x \\ \vdots \\ v_m\cdot x \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Therefore, \(x\) is in \(\Nul(A)\) if and only if \(x\) is perpendicular to each vector \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Since column spaces are the same as spans, we can rephrase the proposition as follows. Let \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\) be vectors in \(\R^n\text{,}\) and let \(W = \Span\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{gather*} W^\perp = \bigl\{\text{all vectors orthogonal to each $v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m$}\bigr\} = \Nul\begin{pmatrix} \matrow{v_1^T} \\ \matrow{v_2^T} \\ \vdots \\ \matrow{v_m^T} \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Again, it is important to be able to go easily back and forth between spans and column spaces. If you are handed a span, you can apply the proposition once you have rewritten your span as a column space.
    🔗
    By the proposition, computing the orthogonal complement of a span means solving a system of linear equations. For example, if
    \begin{gather*} v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 7 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \qquad v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \end{gather*}
    then \(\Span\{v_1,v_2\}^\perp\) is the solution set of the homogeneous linear system associated to the matrix
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} \matrow{v_1^T} \\ \matrow{v_2^T} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 7 \amp 2 \\ -2 \amp 3 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    This is the solution set of the system of equations
    \begin{align*} \left\{\begin{alignedat}{4} x_1 \amp {}+{} \amp 7x_2 \amp {}+{} \amp 2x_3 \amp {}={} \amp 0 \amp {} \\ -2x_1 \amp {}+{} \amp 3x_2 \amp {}+{} \amp x_3 \amp {}={} \amp 0 \amp . \end{alignedat}\right. \end{align*}
    🔗

    Example 6.2.11.

    Compute \(W^\perp\text{,}\) where
    \begin{gather*} W = \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 7 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    According to the Proposition 10, we need to compute the null space of the matrix
    \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 7 \amp 2 \\ -2 \amp 3 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{\text{RREF}}\; \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp -1/17 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 5/17 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The free variable is \(x_3\text{,}\) so the parametric form of the solution set is \(x_1=x_3/17,\,x_2=-5x_3/17\text{,}\) and the parametric vector form is
    \begin{gather*} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix} = x_3\begin{pmatrix} 1/17 \\ -5/17 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Scaling by a factor of \(17\text{,}\) we see that
    \begin{gather*} W^\perp = \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -5 \\ 17 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    We can check our work:
    \begin{gather*} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 7 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -5 \\ 17 \end{pmatrix} = 0 \qquad \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -5 \\ 17 \end{pmatrix} = 0\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.2.12.

    Find all vectors orthogonal to \(v = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}.\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    According to the Proposition 10, we need to compute the null space of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} \matrow{v} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    This matrix is in reduced-row echelon form. The parametric form for the solution set is \(x_1 = -x_2 + x_3\text{,}\) so the parametric vector form of the general solution is
    \begin{gather*} x = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix} = x_2\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + x_3\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Therefore, the answer is the plane
    \begin{gather*} \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.2.14.

    Compute
    \begin{gather*} \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\right\}^\perp\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    According to the Proposition 10, we need to compute the null space of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\;\xrightarrow{\text{RREF}}\;\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The parametric vector form of the solution is
    \begin{gather*} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix} = x_2\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Therefore, the answer is the line
    \begin{gather*} \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In order to find shortcuts for computing orthogonal complements, we need the following basic facts. Looking back the the above examples, all of these facts should be believable.
    🔗

    Fact 6.2.16 Facts about Orthogonal Complements.

    Let \(W\) be a subspace of \(\R^n\text{.}\) Then:
    1. \(W^\perp\) is also a subspace of \(\R^n.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle (W^\perp)^\perp = W.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \dim(W) + \dim(W^\perp) = n.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    For the first assertion, we verify the three defining properties of subspaces.
    1. The zero vector is in \(W^\perp\) because the zero vector is orthogonal to every vector in \(\R^n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Let \(u,v\) be in \(W^\perp\text{,}\) so \(u\cdot x = 0\) and \(v\cdot x = 0\) for every vector \(x\) in \(W\text{.}\) We must verify that \((u+v)\cdot x = 0\) for every \(x\) in \(W\text{.}\) Indeed, we have
      \begin{gather*} (u+v)\cdot x = u\cdot x + v\cdot x = 0 + 0 = 0\text{.} \end{gather*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Let \(u\) be in \(W^\perp\text{,}\) so \(u\cdot x = 0\) for every \(x\) in \(W\text{,}\) and let \(c\) be a scalar. We must verify that \((cu)\cdot x = 0\) for every \(x\) in \(W\text{.}\) Indeed, we have
      \begin{gather*} (cu)\cdot x = c(u\cdot x) = c0 = 0\text{.} \end{gather*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Next we prove the third assertion. Let \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\) be a basis for \(W\text{,}\) so \(m = \dim(W)\text{,}\) and let \(v_{m+1},v_{m+2},\ldots,v_k\) be a basis for \(W^\perp\text{,}\) so \(k-m = \dim(W^\perp)\text{.}\) We need to show \(k=n\text{.}\) First we claim that \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m,v_{m+1},v_{m+2},\ldots,v_k\}\) is linearly independent. Suppose that \(c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_kv_k = 0\text{.}\) Let \(w = c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_mv_m\) and \(w' = c_{m+1}v_{m+1} + c_{m+2}v_{m+2} + \cdots + c_kv_k\text{,}\) so \(w\) is in \(W\text{,}\) \(w'\) is in \(W'\text{,}\) and \(w + w' = 0\text{.}\) Then \(w = -w'\) is in both \(W\) and \(W^\perp\text{,}\) which implies \(w\) is perpendicular to itself. In particular, \(w\cdot w = 0\text{,}\) so \(w = 0\text{,}\) and hence \(w' = 0\text{.}\) Therefore, all coefficients \(c_i\) are equal to zero, because \(\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}\) and \(\{v_{m+1},v_{m+2},\ldots,v_k\}\) are linearly independent.
    🔗
    It follows from the previous paragraph that \(k \leq n\text{.}\) Suppose that \(k \lt n\text{.}\) Then the matrix
    \begin{gather*} A = \begin{pmatrix} \matrow{v_1^T} \\ \matrow{v_2^T} \\ \vdots \\ \matrow{v_k^T} \end{pmatrix} \end{gather*}
    has more columns than rows (it is “wide”), so its null space is nonzero by Note 14 of Section 3.2. Let \(x\) be a nonzero vector in \(\Nul(A)\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{gather*} 0 = Ax = \begin{pmatrix} v_1^Tx \\ v_2^Tx \\ \vdots \\ v_k^Tx \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} v_1\cdot x \\ v_2\cdot x \\ \vdots \\ v_k\cdot x \end{pmatrix} \end{gather*}
    by the Definition 9 of Section 2.3. Since \(v_1\cdot x = v_2\cdot x = \cdots = v_m\cdot x = 0\text{,}\) it follows from Proposition 10 that \(x\) is in \(W^\perp\text{,}\) and similarly, \(x\) is in \((W^\perp)^\perp\text{.}\) As above, this implies \(x\) is orthogonal to itself, which contradicts our assumption that \(x\) is nonzero. Therefore, \(k = n\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗
    Finally, we prove the second assertion. Clearly \(W\) is contained in \((W^\perp)^\perp\text{:}\) this says that everything in \(W\) is perpendicular to the set of all vectors perpendicular to everything in \(W\text{.}\) Let \(m=\dim(W).\) By 3, we have \(\dim(W^\perp) = n-m\text{,}\) so \(\dim((W^\perp)^\perp) = n - (n-m) = m\text{.}\) The only \(m\)-dimensional subspace of \((W^\perp)^\perp\) is all of \((W^\perp)^\perp\text{,}\) so \((W^\perp)^\perp = W.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    See these Paragraphs  for pictures of the second property. As for the third: for example, if \(W\) is a (\(2\)-dimensional) plane in \(\R^4\text{,}\) then \(W^\perp\) is another (\(2\)-dimensional) plane. Explicitly, we have
    \begin{align*} \Span\bigl\{e_1,e_2\bigr\}^\perp \amp= \left\{\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \\ w \end{pmatrix}\text{ in }\R^4\biggm|\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \\ w \end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = 0 \text{ and } \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \\ w \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = 0\right\}\\ \amp= \left\{\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ z \\ w \end{pmatrix}\text{ in }\R^4\right\} = \Span\bigl\{e_3,e_4\}: \end{align*}
    the orthogonal complement of the \(xy\)-plane is the \(zw\)-plane.
    🔗

    Definition 6.2.17.

    The row space of a matrix \(A\) is the span of the rows of \(A\text{,}\) and is denoted \(\Row(A)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    If \(A\) is an \(m\times n\) matrix, then the rows of \(A\) are vectors with \(n\) entries, so \(\Row(A)\) is a subspace of \(\R^n\text{.}\) Equivalently, since the rows of \(A\) are the columns of \(A^T\text{,}\) the row space of \(A\) is the column space of \(A^T\text{:}\)
    \begin{gather*} \Row(A) = \Col(A^T)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    We showed in the above Proposition 10 that if \(A\) has rows \(v_1^T,v_2^T,\ldots,v_m^T\text{,}\) then
    \begin{gather*} \Row(A)^\perp = \Span\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}^\perp = \Nul(A)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Taking orthogonal complements of both sides and using the Fact 16 gives
    \begin{gather*} \Row(A) = \Nul(A)^\perp\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Replacing \(A\) by \(A^T\) and remembering that \(\Row(A)=\Col(A^T)\) gives
    \begin{gather*} \Col(A)^\perp = \Nul(A^T) \sptxt{and} \Col(A) = \Nul(A^T)^\perp\text{.} \end{gather*}
    To summarize:
    🔗

    Recipes: Shortcuts for computing orthogonal complements.

    For any vectors \(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{gather*} \Span\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_m\}^\perp = \Nul\begin{pmatrix} \matrow{v_1^T} \\ \matrow{v_2^T} \\ \vdots \\ \matrow{v_m^T} \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    For any matrix \(A\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{align*} \begin{aligned} \Row(A)^\perp \amp= \Nul(A) \amp \Nul(A)^\perp \amp= \Row(A) \\ \Col(A)^\perp \amp= \Nul(A^T)\quad \amp \Nul(A^T)^\perp \amp= \Col(A). \end{aligned} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    As mentioned in the beginning of this subsection, in order to compute the orthogonal complement of a general subspace, usually it is best to rewrite the subspace as the column space or null space of a matrix.
    🔗

    Example 6.2.18 Orthogonal complement of a subspace.

    Compute the orthogonal complement of the subspace
    \begin{gather*} W = \bigl\{(x,y,z) \text{ in } \R^3\mid 3x + 2y = z\bigr\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    Rewriting, we see that \(W\) is the solution set of the system of equations \(3x + 2y - z = 0\text{,}\) i.e., the null space of the matrix \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \amp 2 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}.\) Therefore,
    \begin{gather*} W^\perp = \Row(A) = \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    No row reduction was needed!
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.2.19 Orthogonal complement of an eigenspace.

    Find the orthogonal complement of the \(5\)-eigenspace of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \amp 4 \amp -1 \\ 3 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ -2 \amp 4 \amp 3 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    The \(5\)-eigenspace is
    \begin{align*} W = \Nul(A - 5I_3) = \Nul\begin{pmatrix} -3 \amp 4 \amp -1 \\ 3 \amp -3 \amp 0 \\ -2 \amp 4 \amp -2 \end{pmatrix}\text{,} \end{align*}
    so
    \begin{align*} W^\perp = \Row\begin{pmatrix} -3 \amp 4 \amp -1 \\ 3 \amp -3 \amp 0 \\ -2 \amp 4 \amp -2 \end{pmatrix} = \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ 4 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -3 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 4 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{align*}
    These vectors are necessarily linearly dependent (why)?

    Subsection 6.2.3 Row rank and column rank

    Suppose that \(A\) is an \(m \times n\) matrix. Let us refer to the dimensions of \(\Col(A)\) and \(\Row(A)\) as the row rank and the column rank of \(A\) (note that the column rank of \(A\) is the same as the rank of \(A\)). The next theorem says that the row and column ranks are the same. This is surprising for a couple of reasons. First, \(\Row(A)\) lies in \(\R^n\) and \(\Col(A)\) lies in \(\R^m\text{.}\) Also, the theorem implies that \(A\) and \(A^T\) have the same number of pivots, even though the reduced row echelon forms of \(A\) and \(A^T\) have nothing to do with each other otherwise.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.2.20.

    Let \(A\) be a matrix. Then the row rank of \(A\) is equal to the column rank of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    By the Theorem 2 of Section 2.8, we have
    \begin{gather*} \dim\Col(A) + \dim\Nul(A) = n\text{.} \end{gather*}
    On the other hand the Fact 16 says that
    \begin{gather*} \dim\Nul(A)^\perp + \dim\Nul(A) = n\text{,} \end{gather*}
    which implies \(\dim\Col(A) = \dim\Nul(A)^\perp\text{.}\) Since \(\Nul(A)^\perp = \Row(A),\) we have
    \begin{gather*} \dim\Col(A) = \dim\Row(A)\text{,} \end{gather*}
    as desired.
    🔗
    🔗
    In particular, by Corollary 12 of Section 2.7 both the row rank and the column rank are equal to the number of pivots of \(A\text{.}\)
