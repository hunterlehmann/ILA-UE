    Section 5.2 The Characteristic Polynomial

    Objectives

    1. Learn that the eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are the diagonal entries.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find all eigenvalues of a matrix using the characteristic polynomial.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Learn some strategies for finding the zeros of a polynomial.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Recipe: the characteristic polynomial of a \(2\times 2\) matrix.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Vocabulary words: characteristic polynomial, trace.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    In Section 5.1 we discussed how to decide whether a given number \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of a matrix, and if so, how to find all of the associated eigenvectors. In this section, we will give a method for computing all of the eigenvalues of a matrix. This does not reduce to solving a system of linear equations: indeed, it requires solving a nonlinear equation in one variable, namely, finding the roots of the characteristic polynomial.
    🔗

    Definition 5.2.1.

    Let \(A\) be an \(n\times n\) matrix. The characteristic polynomial of \(A\) is the function \(f(\lambda)\) given by
    \begin{gather*} f(\lambda) = \det(A-\lambda I_n)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    We will see Theorem 10 that the characteristic polynomial is in fact a polynomial. Finding the characterestic polynomial means computing the determinant of the matrix \(A-\lambda I_n\text{,}\) whose entries contain the unknown \(\lambda\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 5.2.2.

    Find the characteristic polynomial of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    We have
    \begin{align*} f(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I_2) \amp= \det\left(\begin{pmatrix} 5 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} \lambda \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \lambda \end{pmatrix}\right)\\ \amp= \det\begin{pmatrix} 5-\lambda \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp 1-\lambda \end{pmatrix}\\ \amp= (5-\lambda)(1-\lambda) - 2\cdot 2 = \lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 1\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.2.3.

    Find the characteristic polynomial of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \amp 6 \amp 8 \\ \frac \amp 12 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \frac \amp 12 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    We compute the determinant by expanding cofactors along the third column:
    \begin{align*} f(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I_3) \amp= \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda \amp 6 \amp 8 \\ \frac \amp 12 \amp -\lambda \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \frac \amp 12 \amp -\lambda \end{pmatrix}\\ \amp= 8\left(\frac 14 - 0\cdot-\lambda\right) - \lambda\left(\lambda^2 - 6\cdot\frac 12\right)\\ \amp= -\lambda^3 + 3\lambda + 2\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The point of the characteristic polynomial is that we can use it to compute eigenvalues.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.2.4 Eigenvalues are roots of the characteristic polynomial.

    Let \(A\) be an \(n\times n\) matrix, and let \(f(\lambda) = \det(A-\lambda I_n)\) be its characteristic polynomial. Then a number \(\lambda_0\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) if and only if \(f(\lambda_0) = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    By the Theorem 31 of Section 5.1, the matrix equation \((A-\lambda_0 I_n)x=0\) has a nontrivial solution if and only if \(\det(A-\lambda_0 I_n) = 0\text{.}\) Therefore,
    \begin{align*} \lambda_0 \text{ is an eigenvalue of } A \amp\iff Ax = \lambda_0 x \text{ has a nontrivial solution}\\ \amp\iff (A-\lambda_0 I_n)x = 0 \text{ has a nontrivial solution}\\ \amp\iff A - \lambda_0 I_n \text{ is not invertible}\\ \amp\iff \det(A - \lambda_0 I_n) = 0\\ \amp\iff f(\lambda_0) = 0\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.2.5 Finding eigenvalues.

    Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    In the above Example 2 we computed the characteristic polynomial of \(A\) to be \(f(\lambda) = \lambda^2-6\lambda + 1\text{.}\) We can solve the equation \(\lambda^2-6\lambda+1=0\) using the quadratic formula:
    \begin{gather*} \lambda = \frac{6\pm\sqrt{36-4}}2 = 3\pm 2\sqrt 2\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Therefore, the eigenvalues are \(3+2\sqrt 2\) and \(3-2\sqrt 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To compute the eigenvectors, we solve the homogeneous system of equations \((A-\lambda I_2)x=0\) for each eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{.}\) When \(\lambda=3+2\sqrt 2\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{align*} A-(3+\sqrt 2)I_2 = \amp\begin{pmatrix} 2-2\sqrt2 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp -2-2\sqrt2 \end{pmatrix}\\ \xrightarrow{R_1 = R_1\times(2+2\sqrt2)}\; \amp\begin{pmatrix} -4 \amp 4+4\sqrt2 \\ 2 \amp -2-2\sqrt2 \end{pmatrix}\\ \xrightarrow{R_2 = R_2+R_1/2}\; \amp\begin{pmatrix} -4 \amp 4+4\sqrt2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\\ \xrightarrow{R_1 = R_1 \div -4}\; \amp\begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp -1-\sqrt2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The parametric form of the general solution is \(x=(1+\sqrt 2)y\text{,}\) so the \((3+2\sqrt 2)\)-eigenspace is the line spanned by \({1+\sqrt 2\choose 1}\text{.}\) We compute in the same way that the \((3-2\sqrt 2)\)-eigenspace is the line spanned by \({1-\sqrt 2\choose 1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.2.7 Finding eigenvalues.

    Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \amp 6 \amp 8 \\ \frac \amp 12 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \frac \amp 12 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    In the above Example 3 we computed the characteristic polynomial of \(A\) to be \(f(\lambda) = -\lambda^3+3\lambda+2\text{.}\) We eyeball that \(f(2) = -8 + 3\cdot 2 + 2 = 0\text{.}\) Thus \(\lambda-2\) divides \(f(\lambda)\text{;}\) to find the other roots, we perform polynomial long division:
    \begin{gather*} \frac{-\lambda^3+3\lambda+2}{\lambda-2} = -\lambda^2-2\lambda-1 = -(\lambda+1)^2\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Therefore,
    \begin{gather*} f(\lambda) = -(\lambda-2)(\lambda+1)^2\text{,} \end{gather*}
    so the only eigenvalues are \(\lambda = 2,-1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We compute the \(2\)-eigenspace by solving the homogeneous system \((A-2I_3)x=0\text{.}\) We have
    \begin{align*} A-2I_3 = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \amp 6 \amp 8 \\ \frac \amp 12 \amp -2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \frac \amp 12 \amp -2 \end{pmatrix} \quad\xrightarrow{\text{RREF}}\quad \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp -16 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -4 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The parametric form and parametric vector form of the solutions are:
    \begin{align*} \left\{\begin{alignedat}{2} x \amp {}={} \amp 16z \amp {} \\ y \amp {}={} \amp 4z \amp {} \\ z \amp {}={} \amp z \amp {} \end{alignedat}\right. \quad\To\quad \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix} = z\begin{pmatrix} 16 \\ 4 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Therefore, the \(2\)-eigenspace is the line
    \begin{gather*} \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 16 \\ 4 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    We compute the \(-1\)-eigenspace by solving the homogeneous system \((A+I_3)x=0\text{.}\) We have
    \begin{align*} A+I_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 6 \amp 8 \\ \frac \amp 12 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \frac \amp 12 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix} \quad\xrightarrow{\text{RREF}}\quad \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp -4 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The parametric form and parametric vector form of the solutions are:
    \begin{align*} \left\{\begin{alignedat}{2} x \amp {}={} \amp 4z \amp {} \\ y \amp {}={} \amp -2z \amp {} \\ z \amp {}={} \amp z \amp {} \end{alignedat}\right. \quad\To\quad \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix} = z\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Therefore, the \(-1\)-eigenspace is the line
    \begin{gather*} \Span\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Form of the characteristic polynomial.

    It is time that we justified the use of the term “polynomial.” First we need a vocabulary word.
    🔗

    Definition 5.2.9.

    The trace of a square matrix \(A\) is the number \(\Tr(A)\) obtained by summing the diagonal entries of \(A\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \Tr\begin{pmatrix} \amp a_{11} \amp a_{12} \amp \cdots \amp a_{1,n-1} \amp a_{1n} \\ a_{21} \amp \amp a_{22} \amp \cdots \amp a_{2,n-1} \amp a_{2n} \\ \vdots \amp \vdots \amp \ddots \amp \vdots \amp \vdots \\ a_{n-1,1} \amp a_{n-1,2} \amp \cdots \amp \amp a_{n-1,n-1} \amp a_{n-1,n} \\ a_{n1} \amp a_{n2} \amp \cdots \amp a_{n,n-1} \amp \amp a_{nn} \end{pmatrix} = \mh{a_{11}} + a_{22} + \cdots + a_{nn}\color{black}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 5.2.10.

    Let \(A\) be an \(n\times n\) matrix, and let \(f(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I_n)\) be its characteristic polynomial. Then \(f(\lambda)\) is a polynomial of degree \(n\text{.}\) Moreover, \(f(\lambda)\) has the form
    \begin{gather*} f(\lambda) = (-1)^n\lambda^n + (-1)^{n-1}\Tr(A)\lambda^{n-1} + \cdots + \det(A)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    In other words, the coefficient of \(\lambda^{n-1}\) is \(\pm\Tr(A)\text{,}\) and the constant term is \(\det(A)\) (the other coefficients are just numbers without names).
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    First we notice that
    \begin{gather*} f(0) = \det(A - 0I_n) = \det(A)\text{,} \end{gather*}
    so that the constant term is always \(\det(A)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We will prove the rest of the theorem only for \(2\times 2\) matrices; the reader is encouraged to complete the proof in general using cofactor expansions. We can write a \(2\times 2\) matrix as \(A = \smallmat abcd\text{;}\) then
    \begin{align*} f(\lambda) \amp= \det(A-\lambda I_2) = \det\begin{pmatrix} a-\lambda \amp b \\ c \amp d-\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (a-\lambda)(d-\lambda)-bc\\ \amp=\lambda^2 - (a+d)\lambda + (ad-bc) = \lambda^2 - \Tr(A)\lambda + \det(A)\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Recipe 5.2.11 The characteristic polynomial of a \(2\times 2\) matrix.

    When \(n=2\text{,}\) Theorem 10 tells us all of the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial:
    \begin{gather*} f(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - \Tr(A)\lambda + \det(A)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    This is generally the fastest way to compute the characteristic polynomial of a \(2\times 2\) matrix.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.2.12.

    Find the characteristic polynomial of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp 1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    We have
    \begin{gather*} f(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - \Tr(A)\lambda + \det(A) = \lambda^2 - (5+1)\lambda + (5\cdot 1-2\cdot 2) = \lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 1\text{,} \end{gather*}
    as in the above Example 2.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.2.13.

    By the above Theorem 10, the characteristic polynomial of an \(n\times n\) matrix is a polynomial of degree \(n\text{.}\) Since a polynomial of degree \(n\) has at most \(n\) roots, this gives another proof of the fact that an \(n\times n\) matrix has at most \(n\) eigenvalues. See Insight 20 of Section 5.1.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Eigenvalues of a triangular matrix.

    It is easy to compute the determinant of an upper- or lower-triangular matrix; this makes it easy to find its eigenvalues as well.
    🔗

    Corollary 5.2.14.

    If \(A\) is an upper- or lower-triangular matrix, then the eigenvalues of \(A\) are its diagonal entries.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose for simplicity that \(A\) is a \(3\times 3\) upper-triangular matrix:
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} \amp a_{12} \amp a_{13} \\ 0 \amp a_{22} \amp a_{23} \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp a_{33} \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Its characteristic polynomial is
    \begin{align*} f(\lambda) = \det(A-\lambda I_3) = \det\begin{pmatrix} a_{11}-\lambda \amp a_{12} \amp a_{13} \\ 0 \amp a_{22}-\lambda \amp a_{23} \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp a_{33}-\lambda \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    This is also an upper-triangular matrix, so the determinant is the product of the diagonal entries:
    \begin{gather*} f(\lambda) = (a_{11}-\lambda)(a_{22}-\lambda)(a_{33}-\lambda)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    The zeros of this polynomial are exactly \(a_{11},\,a_{22},\,a_{33}.\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.2.15.

    Find the eigenvalues of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \amp 7 \amp 2 \amp 4 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 3 \amp 11 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp \pi \amp 101 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    The eigenvalues are the diagonal entries \(1,\pi,0\text{.}\) (The eigenvalue \(1\) occurs twice, but it counts as one eigenvalue; in Section 5.3 we will define the notion of algebraic multiplicity of an eigenvalue.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Factoring the characteristic polynomial.

    If \(A\) is an \(n\times n\) matrix, then the characteristic polynomial \(f(\lambda)\) has degree \(n\) by the above Theorem 10. When \(n=2\text{,}\) one can use the quadratic formula to find the roots of \(f(\lambda)\text{.}\) There exist algebraic formulas for the roots of cubic and quartic polynomials, but these are generally too cumbersome to apply by hand. Even worse, it is known that there is no algebraic formula for the roots of a general polynomial of degree at least \(5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In practice, the roots of the characteristic polynomial are found numerically by computer. That said, there do exist methods for finding roots by hand. For instance, we have the following consequence of the rational root theorem (which we also call the rational root theorem):
    🔗

    Fact 5.2.16.

    Suppose that \(A\) is an \(n\times n\) matrix whose characteristic polynomial \(f(\lambda)\) has integer (whole-number) entries. Then all rational roots of its characteristic polynomial are integer divisors of \(\det(A)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    For example, if \(A\) has integer entries, then its characteristic polynomial has integer coefficients. This gives us one way to find a root by hand, if \(A\) has an eigenvalue that is a rational number. Once we have found one root, then we can reduce the degree by polynomial long division.
    🔗

    Example 5.2.17.

    Find the eigenvalues of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \amp 0 \amp 3 \\ -3 \amp 2 \amp -3 \\ -3 \amp 0 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Hint: one eigenvalue is an integer.
    🔗

    Solution.

    We compute the characteristic polynomial by expanding cofactors along the first row:
    \begin{align*} f(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I_3) \amp= \det\begin{pmatrix} 7-\lambda \amp 0 \amp 3 \\ -3 \amp 2-\lambda \amp -3 \\ -3 \amp 0 \amp -1-\lambda \end{pmatrix}\\ \amp= (7-\lambda)(2-\lambda)(-1-\lambda) + 3\cdot 3(2-\lambda)\\ \amp= -\lambda^3 + 8\lambda^2 - 14\lambda + 4\text{.} \end{align*}
    The determinant of \(A\) is the constant term \(f(0) = 4\text{;}\) its integer divisors are \(\pm 1,\pm 2,\pm 4\text{.}\) We check which are roots:
    \begin{gather*} f(1) = -3 \quad f(-1) = 27 \quad f(2) = 0 \quad f(-2) = 72 \quad f(4) = 12 \quad f(-4) = 252\text{.} \end{gather*}
    The only rational root of \(f(\lambda)\) is \(\lambda=2\text{.}\) We divide by \(\lambda-2\) using polynomial long division:
    \begin{gather*} \frac{-\lambda^3 + 8\lambda^2 - 14\lambda + 4}{\lambda-2} = -\lambda^2 + 6\lambda - 2\text{.} \end{gather*}
    We can use the quadratic formula to find the roots of the quotient:
    \begin{gather*} \lambda = \frac{-6 \pm\sqrt{36 - 4\cdot 2}}{-2} = 3\pm\sqrt 7\text{.} \end{gather*}
    We have factored \(f\) completely:
    \begin{gather*} f(\lambda) = -(\lambda-2)\bigl(\lambda-(3+\sqrt7)\bigr)\bigl(\lambda-(3-\sqrt7)\bigr)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Therefore, the eigenvalues of \(A\) are \(2,\,3+\sqrt7,\,3-\sqrt7.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In the above example, we could have expanded cofactors along the second column to obtain
    \begin{align*} f(\lambda) = (2-\lambda)\det\begin{pmatrix} 7-\lambda \amp 3 \\ -3 \amp -1-\lambda \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Since \(2-\lambda\) was the only nonzero entry in its column, this expression already has the \(2-\lambda\) term factored out: the rational root theorem was not needed. The determinant in the above expression is the characteristic polynomial of the matrix \(\smallmat 73{-3}{-1}\text{,}\) so we can compute it using the trace and determinant:
    \begin{gather*} f(\lambda) = (2-\lambda)\bigl(\lambda^2-(7-1)\lambda+(-7+9)\bigr) = (2-\lambda)(\lambda^2-6\lambda+2)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗

    Example 5.2.18.

    Find the eigenvalues of the matrix
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \amp 0 \amp 3 \\ -3 \amp 2 \amp -3 \\ 4 \amp 2 \amp 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    We compute the characteristic polynomial by expanding cofactors along the first row:
    \begin{align*} f(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I_3) \amp= \det\begin{pmatrix} 7-\lambda \amp 0 \amp 3 \\ -3 \amp 2-\lambda \amp -3 \\ 4 \amp 2 \amp -\lambda \end{pmatrix}\\ \amp= (7-\lambda)\bigl(-\lambda(2-\lambda)+6\bigr) + 3\bigl(-6-4(2-\lambda)\bigr)\\ \amp= -\lambda^3 + 9\lambda^2 - 8\lambda\text{.} \end{align*}
    The constant term is zero, so \(A\) has determinant zero. We factor out \(\lambda\text{,}\) then eyeball the roots of the quadratic factor:
    \begin{gather*} f(\lambda) = -\lambda(\lambda^2-9\lambda+8) = -\lambda(\lambda-1)(\lambda-8)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Therefore, the eigenvalues of \(A\) are \(0,1,\) and \(8\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Insight 5.2.19 Finding Eigenvalues of a Matrix Larger than \(2\times 2\).

    Let \(A\) be an \(n\times n\) matrix. Here are some strategies for factoring its characteristic polynomial \(f(\lambda)\text{.}\) First, you must find one eigenvalue:
    1. Do not multiply out the characteristic polynomial if it is already partially factored! This happens if you expand cofactors along the second column in Example 17.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If there is no constant term, you can factor out \(\lambda\text{,}\) as in Example 18.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. If the matrix is triangular, the roots are the diagonal entries.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Guess one eigenvalue using the Fact 16: if \(\det(A)\) is an integer, substitute all (positive and negative) divisors of \(\det(A)\) into \(f(\lambda)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Find an eigenvalue using the geometry of the matrix. For instance, a reflection has eigenvalues \(\pm 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    After obtaining an eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\text{,}\) use polynomial long division to compute \(f(\lambda)/(\lambda-\lambda_1)\text{.}\) This polynomial has lower degree. If \(n=3\) then this is a quadratic polynomial, to which you can apply the quadratic formula to find the remaining roots.

    5.

    What are the eigenvalues and eigenvectors for the \(n \times n\) zero matrix?
    🔗
    \ifanswers \newpage \fi
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(A\) be the \(n \times n\) zero matrix. Then \(A-\lambda I\) is the \(n \times n\) diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are all \(-\lambda\text{,}\) so its characteristic polynomial is \((-\lambda)^n\text{.}\) Therefore, the only eigenvalue is \(\lambda = 0\text{.}\) The \(0\)-eigenspace is all of \({\bf R}^{n}\text{,}\) since \(Ax = 0\) for every \(x\) in \({\bf R}^{n}\text{.}\)

    6.

    Find the eigenvalues of \(\begin{pmatrix}1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp -5 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 8 \amp 0\end{pmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We solve for \(0 = \det(A-\lambda I)\) using the cofactor expansion along the second row.
    \begin{align*} 0 \amp = \det \begin{pmatrix}1-\lambda \amp \ \amp 2 \amp 1\\ 0 \amp -5-\lambda \amp \ \amp 0\\ 1 \amp 8 \amp -\lambda\end{pmatrix} = (-1)^{4}(-5-\lambda) \det\begin{pmatrix}1-\lambda \amp \ \amp 1\\ 1 \amp -\lambda\end{pmatrix}\\ \amp = (-5-\lambda)\Big(\lambda^2-\lambda-1\Big)\text{.} \end{align*}
    Thus, \(\lambda = -5\) is an eigenvalue, and so are the solutions to \(\lambda^2-\lambda -1 = 0\text{.}\) For this, we use the quadratic formula to get
    \begin{gather*} \lambda = \frac{-(-1) \pm \sqrt{(-1)^2-4(-1)(1)}}{2} = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    The eiegenvalues are \(\lambda = -5\text{,}\) \(\lambda = \dfrac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}\text{,}\) and \(\lambda = \dfrac{1 - \sqrt{5}}{2}\text{.}\)

    7.

    Find the eigenvalues of \(A = \begin{pmatrix}9 \amp 9 \\ 9 \amp 9\end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) For each eigenvalue, find a basis for the corresponding eigenspace.
    🔗
    \ifanswers \newpage \fi
    🔗
    Solution.
    The characteristic polynomial can be computed by doing the full computation of \(\det(A- \lambda I)\) or using the shortcut \(2 \times 2\) formula. It is
    \begin{gather*} \det(A-\lambda I) = \lambda^2 - \textup{Tr}(A) \lambda + \det(A) = \lambda^2 - 18 \lambda + 0 = \lambda(\lambda-18)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Therefore, the eigenvalues are \(\lambda = 0\) and \(\lambda = 18\text{.}\) We row-reduce
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}A-0I \amp \ \amp 0\end{array}\right)= \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}9 \amp 9 \amp 0 \\ 9 \amp 9 \amp 0\end{array}\right) \to \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0\end{array}\right)\text{,} \end{align*}
    so \(\left\{\begin{pmatrix}-1 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\right\}\) is a basis for the \(0\)-eigenspace. Also,
    \begin{align*} \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}A-18I \amp \ \amp 0\end{array}\right)= \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}-9 \amp 9 \amp 0 \\ 9 \amp -9 \amp 0\end{array}\right) \to \left(\begin{array}{rr|r}1 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0\end{array}\right)\text{,} \end{align*}
    so \(\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}\right\}\) is a basis for the \(18\)-eigenspace.

    8.

    Consider the matrix \(A\) for rotation by \(\pi/2\) radians about the \(z\)-axis in \({\bf R}^3\text{.}\) What are its real eigenvalues?
    🔗
    Solution.
    We can think of \(A\) as the matrix that rotates the “xy-piece” of the vector by \(\pi/2\) radians, while keeping the \(z\)-coordinate fixed. This means
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix}0 \amp -1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Its characteristic polynomial is the following, which we compute using the cofactor expansion along the third row.
    \begin{align*} \det(A - \lambda I) \amp = \det\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda \amp \ \amp -1 \amp 0\\ 1 \amp -\lambda \amp \ \amp 0\\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1-\lambda\end{pmatrix} = (-1)^{3+3}(1-\lambda)\Big[(-\lambda)(-\lambda)-1(-1)\Big]\\ \amp = (1-\lambda)(\lambda^2 + 1)\text{.} \end{align*}
    The characteristic polynomial factors as \((1-\lambda)(\lambda+i)(\lambda-i)\text{,}\) so the only real eigenvalue is \(\lambda = 1\text{.}\) Complex eigenvalues will be covered in a later section of the textbook.

    10.

    Let \(A\) be the \(2 \times 2\) matrix satisfying
    \begin{gather*} A\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-2 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad A \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    What are the eigenvalues of \(A\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We find \(A\) first. Since \(\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 3\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 3\end{pmatrix}\text{,}\) linearity gives us
    \begin{gather*} A\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{pmatrix} = A \left(\begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 3\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 3\end{pmatrix}\right) = A \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 3\end{pmatrix} - 3 A \begin{pmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-2 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} - 3 \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-5 \\ -5\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Therefore,
    \begin{align*} A = \begin{pmatrix}A\begin{pmatrix}1 \amp \\ \amp 0\end{pmatrix} \amp A \amp \begin{pmatrix}0 \amp \\ \amp 1\end{pmatrix}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-5 \amp 1 \\ -5 \amp 2\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    For the eigenvalues of \(A\text{,}\) we solve:
    \begin{align*} 0 \amp = \det(A-\lambda I) = \lambda^2- \Tr(A)\lambda + \det(A)\\ \amp = \lambda^2 + 3 \lambda -5,\\ \amp \lambda = \dfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt{9-4(-5)(1)}}{2} = \dfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt{29}}{2}\text{.} \end{align*}

    11.

    Find the eigenvalues of the matrix \(A\) that first reflects vectors in \({\bf R}^2\) across the line \(y = -x\text{,}\) then rotates \(\pi/2\) radians counterclockwise, then scales the first coordinate by a factor of \(4\) while keeping the second coordinate fixed.
    🔗
    Solution.
    One way to do this problem is to compute \(A\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} A \amp = \begin{pmatrix}4 \amp 0\\ 0 \amp 1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}0 \amp -1\\ 1 \amp 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}0 \amp -1\\ -1 \amp 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 \amp -4\\ 1 \amp 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}0 \amp -1\\ -1 \amp 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}4 \amp 0\\ 0 \amp -1\end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Therefore, the eigenvalues of \(A\) are \(\lambda = 4\) and \(\lambda = -1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Alternatively, we could have computed \(Ae_1\) and \(Ae_2\) geometrically and found that \(Ae_1 = 4 e_1\) and \(Ae_2 = -e_2\text{,}\) so \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) are eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalues \(4\) and \(-1\) respectively. Since a \(2 \times 2\) matrix has at most \(2\) distinct eigenvalues, these are the only eigenvalues of \(A\text{.}\)

    12.

    Suppose \(A\) and \(B\) are \(n \times n\) matrices with the same characteristic polynomial. Do \(A\) and \(B\) have the same eigenvalues? Answer sometimes, always, or never, and justify your answer.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Always. The eigenvalues of a square matrix are the roots of its characteristic polynomial, so if \(A\) and \(B\) have the same characteristic polynomial then their eigenvalues will be the same.
